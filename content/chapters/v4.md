{at Virginia · 1924} Who counts as Black depends on who is counting. In 1924 [[what-black-means|Virginia's Racial Integrity Act]] called white only a person with "no trace whatsoever of any blood other than Caucasian". Brazil's 1976 household survey, which let people name their own colour, recorded 136 terms, from morena to clara.

{at Russia · 1705} The diaspora reached the Russian court. Around 1705 a kidnapped African boy was brought to Peter the Great, and the tsar became his godfather and sent him to France to study military engineering. As [[gannibal|Abram Petrovich Gannibal]] he rose to general-in-chief; his great-grandson was Alexander Pushkin.

{at Salvador · c. 1830} In Brazil the diaspora built temples of its own. In Salvador, around 1830, three African women founded a house for the orixás behind the Barroquinha church, and from it descend [[candomble|the city's most famous candomblé temples]]. When the anthropologist Ruth Landes worked in them in 1938–39, she found them led by women.

{at Brazil · 1890} Brazil also kept a fight that looks like a dance. [[capoeira|Capoeira]] is played in a circle to the single-string berimbau. Brazil's 1890 penal code sent its players to prison; in 2014 UNESCO inscribed the capoeira circle as intangible heritage.

{at Rio de Janeiro · 1916} Migrants carried [[samba|samba de roda]] south to Rio, where Bahian "aunts" led the Black neighbourhood around Praça Onze. At the house of Tia Ciata a song called Pelo Telefone took shape; Donga registered it in 1916, though other regulars said the tune was collective.

{at Trinidad · 1951} In Trinidad the colonial government banned drumming, then bamboo too, so in Port of Spain young men hammered discarded oil drums into tuned notes and made [[steelpan|the steelpan]]. In 1951 a Trinidad steel orchestra played the Festival of Britain, and in 2024 Parliament voted the steelpan the national instrument.

{at São Paulo · 1960} In São Paulo, [[carolina-maria-de-jesus|Carolina Maria de Jesus]], who had two years of school, collected waste paper for a living and raised three children in a favela shack. And she wrote. In 1960 her diary came out as Quarto de Despejo, "Room of Garbage".

{at [[liberia|Liberia]] · 1830s} The story turns back a century here, to Africa itself, which was writing its own languages down. In the early 1830s, in what is now western Liberia, Mọmọlu Duwalu Bukẹlẹ gave the Vai language [[vai-script|a script of its own]]. The idea, he later said, came to him in a dream, in which a messenger showed him a book. The script went to work at once, in letters, accounts and records of trade.

{at Liberia · 1847} On that coast part of the diaspora came back. Settlers sent by the American Colonization Society first sailed in 1820, and in 1847 [[liberia|Liberia]] became a republic. In 1930 an international commission found its officials shipping forced labour to Fernando Po, in conditions "scarcely distinguishable from slave raiding".

{at Foumban · c. 1910} In Cameroon a king gave his people a script by design. Ibrahim Njoya began [[bamum|the Bamum script]] with hundreds of signs for things and actions and, through six revisions, simplified it into a working system, finished around 1910. In Foumban it served for letters, records, medicine and a history of his people. In Europe, meanwhile, Black scholars and musicians had been making their names for centuries.
