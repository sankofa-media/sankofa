Here the story becomes a count. Between 1500 and 1866 traders forced 12.5 million Africans aboard ships, and [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]], a database begun in 1999, records the voyages one by one. It also keeps names: nearly 100,000 Africans freed from slave ships, each listed as a person.

Every ship needed sellers on the shore. In the Bight of Biafra, which had no large state, [[aro-and-dahomey|the Aro merchants of Arochukwu]] drove the trade deep into Igboland. In 1999 Benin's president, Mathieu Kérékou, whose country was once Dahomey, called a conference to ask forgiveness.

From Igbo country, by his own account, came a kidnapped boy who would answer in print. [[equiano|Olaudah Equiano]] bought his freedom in 1766 for £40, and his life story, published in 1789, became a bestseller.

He carried other stories too. In late 1781 the crew of the slave ship [[zong|Zong]], having overshot Jamaica, threw more than 130 captives into the sea, and the owners asked their insurers to pay for them as goods lost. In 1783 a jury found for the owners.

Equiano took the news to [[granville-sharp|Granville Sharp]], a clerk at the Tower of London who had fought slavery since 1765, when he took in Jonathan Strong, a young man beaten and thrown into the street by his master. Sharp demanded a murder trial. He never got one.

Abolitionists turned to pictures. In 1788 they commissioned a plan of how people were packed aboard the Liverpool ship Brooks; printed in London as [[brookes|Description of a Slave Ship]], it showed rows of bodies inside a hull.

A second image could be worn. In 1787 Josiah Wedgwood made [[wedgwood-medallion|a jasperware cameo]] of a kneeling man in chains asking "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?" and gave it away; in 1788 he sent a batch to Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia.

In Virginia, one African life had taken another turn. A man listed in 1621 as "Antonio, a Negro" won his freedom and, as [[anthony-johnson|Anthony Johnson]], became a landowner with 250 acres. In 1655 a court returned his own servant, John Casor, to him.

Then [[partus-1662|the law]] closed a door. In 1656 Elizabeth Key, daughter of an English planter and an enslaved African woman, won her freedom, because English common law said a child followed the father. In 1662 Virginia enacted that children would be "bond or free only according to the condition of the mother".

The United States banned the trade, and half a century later a ship still sailed. In 1860 the schooner [[clotilda|Clotilda]] brought captives from Ouidah to Alabama, and its captain burned it to hide the evidence. Freed in 1865, the survivors bought land and built African Town.
