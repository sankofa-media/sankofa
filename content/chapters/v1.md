{at Atlantic · 1500–1866} Here the story becomes a count. Between 1500 and 1866 traders forced 12.5 million Africans aboard ships, and [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]], a database begun in 1999, records the voyages one by one. It also keeps names: nearly 100,000 Africans freed from slave ships, each listed as a person.

{at Virginia · 1621} At first the English colonies had not yet fixed their rules. A man listed in 1621 as "Antonio, a Negro" arrived in Virginia, won his freedom and, as [[anthony-johnson|Anthony Johnson]], became a landowner with 250 acres. In 1655 a court returned his own servant, John Casor, to him.

{at Virginia · 1662} Then [[partus-1662|the law]] closed a door. In 1656 Elizabeth Key, daughter of an English planter and an enslaved African woman, won her freedom, because English common law said a child followed the father. In 1662 Virginia enacted that children would be "bond or free only according to the condition of the mother".

{at Ouidah} Every ship needed sellers on the shore. [[aro-and-dahomey|Dahomey]], a kingdom in what is now Benin, sold captives through its port of Ouidah, where people were sold in the market square and marched down a road to the beach. East of it, in the Bight of Biafra, there was no large state, and the Aro merchants of Arochukwu drove the trade deep into Igboland.

{at Montserrat · 1766} From Igbo country, by his own account, came a kidnapped boy who would answer in print. [[equiano|Olaudah Equiano]] bought his freedom from a Montserrat merchant in 1766 for £40, and his life story, published in 1789, ran to nine editions.

{at Caribbean · 1781} He carried other stories too. In late 1781 the crew of the slave ship [[zong|Zong]], having overshot Jamaica, threw more than 130 captives into the sea, and the owners claimed the dead on their insurance as lost cargo. In 1783 a jury found for the owners.

{at London · 1783} That year Equiano took the news to [[granville-sharp|Granville Sharp]], a clerk at the Tower of London who had fought slavery since 1765, when he took in Jonathan Strong, a young man beaten and thrown into the street by his master. Sharp demanded a murder trial. He never got one.

{at Britain · 1787} British abolitionists turned to pictures. In 1787 their new committee took as its seal a kneeling man in chains asking "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?", and the potter Josiah Wedgwood made it into [[wedgwood-medallion|a jasperware cameo]] and gave it away; in 1788 he sent a batch to Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia.

{at London · 1789} A second image showed the inside of a ship. In 1788 abolitionists commissioned a plan of how people were packed aboard the Liverpool ship Brooks; printed in London in 1789 as [[brookes|Description of a Slave Ship]], it showed rows of bodies laid like cargo.

{at Alabama · 1860} Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and the United States banned the trade too, yet half a century later a ship still sailed. In 1860 the schooner [[clotilda|Clotilda]] brought captives from Ouidah to Alabama, and its captain burned it to hide the evidence. Freed in 1865, the survivors bought land and built African Town. Others had not waited for anyone to free them.
