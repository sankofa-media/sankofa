{at Luanda · 1622} People did not wait to be freed. In 1622 [[nzinga|Njinga]], sister of the king of Ndongo, came to Luanda to make peace with the Portuguese governor. Offered a mat while he sat in a chair, she had an attendant kneel and sat on them, level with him. After decades of war she made peace with Portugal in 1656, and died in 1663, still a queen.

{at Pernambuco · 1695} Runaways from the sugar plantations of Pernambuco built villages of their own. From around 1600, [[palmares|Palmares]] beat back Dutch and Portuguese expeditions for decades; the day its last leader, Zumbi, was killed, 20 November 1695, is now a national holiday in Brazil.

{at Blue Mountains · 1739} In the Blue Mountains of Jamaica, [[nanny-of-the-maroons|Nanny]] led the Windward Maroons against the British. When Captain Quao signed a peace treaty in 1739, she reportedly opposed it.

{at Stono River · 1739} That same year, near [[stono-drum-ban|the Stono River]] in South Carolina, enslaved men marched toward Spanish Florida shouting "Liberty!", some say to the beat of drums. The militia crushed them, and in 1740 the colony restricted enslaved people's drums.

{at Saint-Domingue · 1804} In Saint-Domingue, the enslaved won. They rose in 1791 and beat the troops Napoleon sent to restore slavery, and on 1 January 1804 [[haitian-revolution|Haiti]] was proclaimed. In 1825 France sent the new nation a bill: 150 million francs, to compensate the former planters.

{at Boston · 1829} Words travelled by sea as well. In 1829 a Boston clothes dealer, David Walker, published [[walkers-appeal|his Appeal]], urging the enslaved to resist, and asked sailors heading south to carry copies with them.

{male} {at Fort Sumter · 1862} In the war, freedom also came by water. In May 1862 [[robert-smalls|Robert Smalls]], the enslaved pilot of the Confederate transport Planter, waited until the white crew went ashore, then steamed past Fort Sumter in the captain's hat and handed the ship to the Union Navy. He later bought the house of the man who had enslaved him.

{female} {at Combahee River · 1863} In the war, freedom also came by water. On the night of 1 June 1863 three Union gunboats carrying Black soldiers and Harriet Tubman sailed for [[combahee-raid|the plantations on the Combahee River]], and when they turned back, more than 700 people were free. In 1974 Black feminists in Boston named themselves after the raid: the Combahee River Collective.

{at Philadelphia · 1872} Escape gathered legends. [[underground-railroad-myths|"Follow the Drinking Gourd"]], said to be a sung map, was first printed in 1928, and the quilt code comes from one family's account. Real records exist, though: in Philadelphia William Still logged hundreds of fugitives and in 1872 published their stories.

{at Kumasi · 1900} In Africa the fight went on into a new century. In 1900 the British governor in Kumasi asked the Asante chiefs to hand over the Golden Stool, and [[yaa-asantewaa|Yaa Asantewaa]], Queen Mother of Ejisu, led the rising that followed. She died in exile in the Seychelles in 1921. Others fought with words, and many of their words survive.
