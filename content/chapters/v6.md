{at Bingerville · 1949} In these decades Black people on both sides of the Atlantic won back the right to speak for themselves. Around 1949 Souleymane Kanté, from Kankan in Guinea and living in Bingerville in Côte d'Ivoire, came across the claim in print that Africans had no writing of their own. His answer was [[nko|N'Ko]], and its users still mark 14 April 1949 as Alphabet Day.

{male} {at Alabama · 1932–1972} In the United States, harm was still done quietly, in the name of medicine. From 1932 [[tuskegee|a US Public Health Service study]] followed 399 Black men with syphilis in Macon County, Alabama, and when penicillin became the standard cure, they were not offered it. Only a leak to the press ended the study, in 1972; in 1974 a new law required review boards to approve federally funded research on people.

{female} {at Baltimore · 1951} In the United States, harm was still done quietly, in the name of medicine. In 1951 doctors at Johns Hopkins Hospital, where Black patients lay in a segregated ward, kept a sample of tumour cells from [[henrietta-lacks|Henrietta Lacks]], and no one asked her. Her cells became HeLa, the first immortalised human cell line, used in testing for the polio vaccine, while her family was left out.

{at Houston · 1953} Black music crossed the colour line faster than credit did. In 1953 [[big-mama-thornton|Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton]]'s "Hound Dog", recorded for Don Robey's Peacock label, went to number one on the R&B chart; three years later Elvis Presley's version brought him fame and a fortune. In 1974 she set his millions against her own zero.

{at Mississippi · 1955} Then a mother made the country look. In August 1955 [[emmett-till|Emmett Till]], a Chicago boy of fourteen visiting family in Mississippi, was taken from his great-uncle's house and killed, and an all-white jury acquitted his killers in about an hour. His mother held an open-casket funeral and let Jet photograph her son. That December Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat; she later said she thought of Emmett Till.

{at Lagos · 1950s} In Africa, writers took their stories back. Working in broadcasting in Lagos, [[achebe|Chinua Achebe]] wrote Things Fall Apart to tell an Igbo village's story from the inside, and nearly lost it when the London agency he had mailed the manuscript to went silent. Heinemann printed it in 1958, and it went on to sell more than ten million copies.

{at London · 1959} In Britain the answer was a carnival. Deported from the United States in 1955, the Trinidad-born journalist [[claudia-jones|Claudia Jones]] founded the West Indian Gazette in Brixton, and when race riots broke out in Notting Hill in 1958, she answered in January 1959 with a Caribbean carnival at St Pancras Town Hall. Such indoor carnivals fed the Notting Hill Carnival.

{at Lagos · 1977} Independence did not settle who held power. In 1969 the Lagos bandleader Fela Ransome-Kuti was introduced to the ideas of Malcolm X and Black Power in Los Angeles, and at home he built [[afrobeat|Afrobeat]], dance music made for arguing with power. After his album Zombie attacked the military, about a thousand soldiers stormed his commune in February 1977 and threw his mother from a window.

{at Kamiriithu · 1977} In Kenya, [[ngugi|Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o]] turned against the elites of independence. In 1977 peasants and workers at Kamiriithu staged a Gikuyu play he had co-written, and that December he was arrested and held without charge for a year. In prison he wrote his first Gikuyu novel on toilet paper.

{at Port-au-Prince · 1980} In Haiti the fight was for the airwaves. [[radio-haiti|Radio Haïti-Inter]] was the first station to deliver news in Creole, the language most Haitians speak. On 28 November 1980 the Duvalier dictatorship raided it, and Jean Dominique and Michèle Montas went into exile; when Duvalier fled in 1986, tens of thousands welcomed them home. Others, meanwhile, had begun to imagine another future altogether.
