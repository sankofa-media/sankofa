This is the story told by the people themselves. [[phillis-wheatley|Phillis Wheatley]], brought from Africa as a child and enslaved in Boston, published her poems in London in 1773. Two years later she sent George Washington a poem, and he replied that he would be "happy to see a person so favourd by the Muses".

In New York, in 1827, Samuel Cornish and John Russwurm launched [[freedoms-journal|Freedom's Journal]], the first Black-owned newspaper in the United States. It lasted two years: when Russwurm declared for colonization, readers abandoned it.

{male} Some had to win their voice with their bodies first. In 1834 [[douglass-covey|Frederick Douglass]], about sixteen, was sent to Edward Covey, a farmer known as a "negro-breaker". Douglass fought back, and Covey never laid a hand on him again; four years later he escaped north.

{female} Some had to survive in silence first. In 1835 [[jacobs-garret|Harriet Jacobs]], escaping years of sexual threats, hid in a crawlspace at her grandmother's house in Edenton, North Carolina and for nearly seven years watched her children through a hole. In 1861 she published her story under the name Linda Brent.

Even a voice heard aloud could be rewritten. The 1851 report of [[sojourner-truth|Sojourner Truth]]'s speech in Akron has her say "I am a woman's rights"; "Ain't I a woman?" arrived only in 1863, along with a Southern dialect. Truth had grown up in New York speaking Low Dutch.

Others wrote where no one expected words. In 1834 an enslaved potter in Edgefield, South Carolina, signed and dated a storage jar, and for decades [[dave-the-potter|Dave]] kept writing on his jars: his name, dates and couplets. Freed in the 1860s, he became David Drake.

Objects kept families too. When nine-year-old Ashley was sold, her mother Rose gave her [[ashleys-sack|a cotton sack]]. Generations later Ashley's granddaughter embroidered the story onto it and signed it "Ruth Middleton 1921".

And people sang. [[spirituals|Spirituals]] grew from Bible stories, hymns and African ways of singing. In 1871 nine Fisk University students went on tour hardly singing them, for the songs "were associated with slavery and the dark past"; audiences asked for them, and the spirituals took the singers to Queen Victoria.

[[ida-b-wells|Ida B. Wells]] turned grief into evidence. After her friend Thomas Moss was lynched in Memphis in 1892, she built her case on lynchings reported by white newspapers themselves: "Out of their own mouths shall the murderers be condemned."

In the 1930s someone finally came to ask. Between 1936 and 1938 writers of a federal project recorded the memories of formerly enslaved people, and more than 2,300 of [[wpa-narratives|their accounts]] survive. Most interviewers were white, yet through that filter people who lived through slavery still speak in the first person.
