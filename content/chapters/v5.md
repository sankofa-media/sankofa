{at Granada · 1573} The Harlem Renaissance had a long prehistory: for centuries Black scholars and artists had been making their names in Europe. In Granada an enslaved boy went to grammar classes with his young master and grew up to be [[juan-latino|Juan Latino]], a professor of Latin, whose Latin verse was printed there in 1573. In 1605 Cervantes named him in Don Quixote: "el negro Juan Lati-".

{at Halle · 1729} In 1729 at Halle, [[amo|Anton Wilhelm Amo]], born on the Gold Coast, argued that Africans had the same claim as Europeans not to be enslaved in Europe. That disputation is lost, but in 1734 he took his doctorate at Wittenberg with a treatise on the human mind. Facing racist hostility, he went home to Axim around 1747.

{at Paris · 1781} In Paris, [[saint-georges|Joseph Bologne, Chevalier de Saint-Georges]], born in Guadeloupe, was a celebrated fencer and violinist. In 1781 he founded the Concert de la Loge Olympique, and it was for this orchestra that Haydn wrote his six Paris symphonies.

{at Vienna · 1803} Vienna had its Black virtuoso too. On 24 May 1803 [[bridgetower|George Bridgetower]] and Beethoven gave the first performance of the violin sonata Op. 47. Then their friendship broke, and the printed sonata went to Rodolphe Kreutzer, whose name it still carries.

{at London · 1825} The stage opened as well. [[ira-aldridge|Ira Aldridge]], who could have no real career in New York, made his London debut in 1825 and became famous as Othello, then toured the continent. In St Petersburg in 1858 the poet Taras Shevchenko, born a serf, saw his Othello and drew his portrait.

{at New York · 1892} Then the current turned west. In 1892 [[burleigh-dvorak|Harry T. Burleigh]] came from Erie to the National Conservatory in New York and sang the old plantation songs to Antonín Dvořák; later he set [[spirituals|spirituals]] for voice and piano and carried them into concert halls.

{at Paris · 1900} Black America also answered Europe with data. For the Paris Exposition of 1900, [[du-bois-infographics|W. E. B. Du Bois]] and his Atlanta University students drew charts of Black America by hand, captioned in English and French so that Europe could read them.

{at Tutwiler · 1903} At home, a new music turned trouble into song. Around 1903 W. C. Handy, waiting for a train at Tutwiler, Mississippi, heard a man press a knife to his guitar strings and sing. Handy called it the weirdest music he had ever heard, and went on to write and publish [[blues|blues]] of his own.

{at Chicago · 1917} Then the people moved north, and a newspaper urged them on. [[chicago-defender|The Chicago Defender]] printed the jobs waiting for Black Southerners and in 1917 named 15 May as the start of the "Great Northern Drive"; Black Pullman porters hid bundles of it on southbound trains.

{at New York · 1920} In the northern cities the music reached records, and women carried it. On 10 August 1920 in New York, Mamie Smith, a vaudeville singer and dancer, recorded [[crazy-blues|"Crazy Blues"]] with her Jazz Hounds, and the record opened the "race records" market.

{at Mobile · 1927} The writers went back south to listen. [[hurston|Zora Neale Hurston]], who grew up in Eatonville, Florida, one of the first towns in the United States incorporated by Black people, studied anthropology and went collecting folklore. In Mobile in 1927 she took down the story of Kossola, a survivor of the [[clotilda|Clotilda]], in his own speech. Her book, Barracoon, came out only in 2018.

{at Paris · 1931} The renaissance crossed the ocean back to Europe. In a salon outside Paris, [[nardal-sisters|Paulette Nardal and her sisters]], from Martinique, brought students from the Caribbean and Africa together with Harlem Renaissance writers such as Claude McKay. In 1931 Paulette co-founded La Revue du monde noir, and her guests Césaire and Senghor went on to found Négritude. The next generation took such words from salons into the streets.
