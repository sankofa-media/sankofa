In Granada an enslaved Black boy went to grammar classes with his young master and grew up to be [[juan-latino|Juan Latino]], a professor of Latin. In 1605 Cervantes named him in the prefatory verses of Don Quixote: "el negro Juan Lati-".

In 1729 at Halle, [[amo|Anton Wilhelm Amo]], born on the Gold Coast, argued that Africans had the same claim as Europeans not to be enslaved in Europe. The disputation is lost. Facing racist hostility, he went home to Axim around 1747.

In Paris, [[saint-georges|Joseph Bologne, Chevalier de Saint-Georges]], born in Guadeloupe, was a celebrated fencer and violinist. In 1781 he founded the Concert de la Loge Olympique, and it was for this orchestra that Haydn wrote his six Paris symphonies.

Vienna had its Black virtuoso too. On 24 May 1803 [[bridgetower|George Bridgetower]] and Beethoven gave the first performance of the violin sonata Op. 47. Then their friendship broke, and the printed sonata went to Rodolphe Kreutzer, whose name it still carries.

In New York the spirituals reached a European master. In 1892 [[burleigh-dvorak|Harry T. Burleigh]] came from Erie to the National Conservatory and sang the old plantation songs to Antonín Dvořák; later he set spirituals for voice and piano and carried them into concert halls.

Black America also answered Europe with data. For the Paris Exposition of 1900, [[du-bois-infographics|W. E. B. Du Bois]] and his Atlanta University students drew charts of Black America by hand, captioned in English and French so that Europe could read them.

At home, a new music turned trouble into song. Around 1903 W. C. Handy, waiting for a train at Tutwiler, Mississippi, heard a man press a knife to his guitar strings and sing. Handy called it the weirdest music he had ever heard and built a career publishing such tunes: [[blues|the blues]].

On record, women carried it. On 10 August 1920 in New York, Mamie Smith, a vaudeville singer and dancer, recorded [[crazy-blues|"Crazy Blues"]] with her Jazz Hounds, and the record opened the "race records" market.

The music moved north, and a newspaper urged people to follow. [[chicago-defender|The Chicago Defender]] printed the jobs waiting for Black Southerners and in 1917 named 15 May as the start of the "Great Northern Drive"; Black Pullman porters hid bundles of it on southbound trains.

[[hurston|Zora Neale Hurston]], who grew up in Eatonville, Florida, one of the first towns in the United States incorporated by Black people, went south to collect folklore. In Mobile in 1927 she took down the story of Kossola, a survivor of the [[clotilda|Clotilda]], in his own speech. Her book, Barracoon, came out only in 2018.
