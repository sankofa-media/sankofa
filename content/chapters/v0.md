The story does not start with chains. It starts with a song: early in the thirteenth century [[sunjata|Sunjata Keita]] defeated Sumaoro Kanté, king of Sosso, and founded the Mali Empire. His story still lives in the voices of griots, as a performance rather than a book.

Mali grew rich. In 1324 its ruler, [[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], stopped in Cairo on his way to Mecca with so much gold that its value there fell, as the writer al-Umari recorded. Half a century later a Majorcan mapmaker painted him on the Catalan Atlas, crowned, holding a golden disc.

Mali's scholarship lived on in paper. The families of Timbuktu kept [[timbuktu-manuscripts|their manuscripts]] for centuries, and when armed jihadist groups took the city in 2012, librarians got the books out to Bamako, some on carts, some by boat on the Niger.

Knowledge also travelled without paper. Yoruba is a tonal language, and [[talking-drums|the talking drums]] copy its tones: squeezing the cords of the hourglass dùndún tightens its skins, so the drum follows the melody of speech. What it says is praise poetry, proverbs and the praise names of important people.

Cloth could speak as well. In Bonwire, the Asante say, two men learned to weave by watching a spider at its web, and [[kente|kente]] designs carry names taken from proverbs, sayings and events, so a cloth can be read as well as worn.

Its kin, [[adinkra|adinkra]] cloth, is stamped rather than woven, and each sign stands for a proverb or an idea. One is a bird looking back over its shoulder: Sankofa, "go back and get it", heritage as a tool to build the future. A stamped cloth acquired in 1817 is now in the British Museum.

In the Cross River region, signs kept secrets. [[nsibidi|Nsibidi]] stands for ideas, not sounds, and its deepest meanings belong to the initiates of the Ekpe, the leopard society. From the 1830s enslaved Cross River Africans rebuilt Ekpe lodges in Cuba as the Abakuá society.

In Benin City the Oba's royal guild cast [[benin-bronzes|the Benin Bronzes]] in lost wax, pouring molten metal where a wax model had melted away. Newcomers from the sea appear on the palace plaques too: Portuguese, shown as foreign merchants.

The Europeans brought a currency from the far side of the world: [[cowries|cowrie shells]], gathered in the Maldives on coconut-leaf mats laid on the water. From the sixteenth century to the nineteenth, ships carried them to the Bight of Benin, where they were paid directly for captives. Here the chains begin.
