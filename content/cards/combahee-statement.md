The Combahee River Collective Statement is a manifesto dated April 1977, written by a group of Black feminists in Boston who felt passed over twice: by a women's movement blind to racism and by Black political groups blind to sexism.

The collective formed in 1974, when a few women broke away from the Boston chapter of the National Black Feminist Organization. Its founders were Barbara Smith, her sister Beverly Smith and Demita Frazier, who had been a member of the Black Panther Party in Chicago. Barbara Smith owned a small book about Harriet Tubman's 1863 [[combahee-raid|raid on the Combahee River]], and the group took its name from it, honouring Tubman and signalling that liberation takes political action.

The statement was written for an anthology edited by Zillah Eisenstein. Its authors saw "the major systems of oppression" as "interlocking", and they called their approach identity politics: "the most profound and potentially most radical politics come directly out of our own identity."

In 1979, as a series of murders of Black women shook Boston, the collective put out pamphlets about the killings and marched in protest. The group stopped meeting around 1980. The phrase it put into circulation, identity politics, has since become one of the most argued-over terms in American politics.
