The Luba kingdom grew up in what is now the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo, and from the 16th century until colonial rule it was one of the most powerful states of central Africa. Its history was not kept in books. It was recited, and the lukasa was the reciter's instrument.

A lukasa is a hand-held wooden board, narrow at the waist so that it fits the palm. Its face is studded with beads, shells and pegs or carved with signs. Large beads ringed by small ones, lines and clusters stand for chiefs, places and events. The board belonged to the Mbudye, a society charged with keeping and interpreting the kingdom's history, whose members carried real political weight beside the kings. Only members at the top of the society could read a lukasa. Holding it in one hand, the reader traced its surface with the fingers of the other and recited the founding of the kingdom, the line of its kings and the boundaries of its chiefdoms.

The kingdom was broken up under Belgian colonial rule, and many boards left Africa for museums in New York, Ohio and beyond. Each board is unique: not a text that anyone could read, but a key that unlocks a trained memory.
