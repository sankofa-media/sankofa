Two laws, signed in Washington thirteen months apart, outlawed segregation in American public life and tore down the legal barriers to the Black vote. In June 1963 President John F. Kennedy sent Congress a broad civil rights bill, and in August the [[march-on-washington|March on Washington]] rallied behind it. After Kennedy's assassination, Lyndon B. Johnson pressed it on. Southern senators tried to talk it to death in a filibuster until the minority leader, Everett Dirksen, gathered enough Republican votes to cut off debate.

On 2 July 1964 Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act, with [[martin-luther-king|Martin Luther King Jr]]. among the guests. It outlawed segregation in hotels, restaurants, theatres and other public places, banned discrimination in employment, including on grounds of sex, and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

The ballot was still blocked by literacy tests and intimidation. After troopers attacked marchers at [[selma-1965|Selma]] in March 1965, Johnson sent Congress a voting rights bill and signed it on 6 August. The Voting Rights Act banned literacy tests, sent federal examiners to register voters in places with a record of discrimination, and made those places get federal approval before changing their election rules. In 1966 the Supreme Court struck down Virginia's poll tax. In 2013, in Shelby County v. Holder, the Court voided the formula that decided which places needed that approval.
