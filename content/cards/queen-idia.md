Idia lived in the kingdom of Benin, in what is now southern Nigeria, at the start of the sixteenth century. Her son Esigie, then oba, fought wars that expanded the kingdom, and Benin remembers Idia as the force behind his campaigns, a woman who went to war herself. To honour her, Esigie created a new title, Iyoba, queen mother, and Idia was the first to hold it.

After her death Esigie had masks made in her memory, among them ivory pendants of her face, made to hang on the chest or at the waist. In 1897, when British troops sacked Benin City, one of these masks was seized from the palace along with the royal treasures now known as [[benin-bronzes|the Benin Bronzes]]; it is now in the British Museum. A second ivory pendant mask of Idia is in the Metropolitan Museum in New York.

In 1977 Nigeria hosted FESTAC, the Second World Black and African Festival of Arts and Culture, and chose the London mask as its emblem. Nigeria asked the British Museum to return it, or at least lend it for the festival; the museum refused, citing its fragility. Nigeria made its own copies, and Idia's face filled Lagos on banners, brooches and flags. The original is still in London.
