Idia lived in the kingdom of Benin, in what is now southern Nigeria, at the start of the sixteenth century. Her son Esigie, then oba, fought wars that expanded the kingdom, and Benin gave Idia a decisive share of the credit: she is remembered as a powerful, politically astute woman who stood behind his campaigns. To honour her, Esigie created a new title, Iyoba, queen mother, which Benin's queen mothers have held ever since.

Esigie is said to have worn ivory pendant masks of her face, on his chest or at his waist, in rites for her. In 1897, when British troops sacked Benin City, one of these masks was seized from the palace along with the royal treasures now known as [[benin-bronzes|the Benin Bronzes]]; it is now in the British Museum. A second ivory mask believed to portray her is in the Metropolitan Museum in New York.

In 1977 Nigeria hosted FESTAC, the Second World Black and African Festival of Arts and Culture, and chose the London mask as its emblem. Nigeria asked the British Museum to return it, or at least lend it for the festival; the museum refused, saying it was too fragile to travel. Nigeria made its own copies, and Idia's face filled Lagos on banners, brooches and flags. The original is still in London.
