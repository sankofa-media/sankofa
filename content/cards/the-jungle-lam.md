The Jungle is a large painting by the Cuban artist Wifredo Lam. He was born in 1902 in Sagua la Grande, the son of a Chinese immigrant father and a mother of African and Spanish descent. In 1938 he reached Paris and met Picasso, and soon he joined André Breton's Surrealists. The war drove him out: in 1941 he sailed from Marseille and made his way back to Havana.

Home again, he looked hard at Afro-Cuban life and at Santería, the faith of the orishas, which he had known since childhood. At the end of 1942 he began a huge painting on paper, nearly two and a half metres high, and finished it in 1943. In The Jungle hybrid figures, part human and part animal, stand among thick, banded stalks of sugar cane, the crop enslaved Africans were brought to Cuba to cut. The jungle is a Caribbean one, and the plantation is in it.

Lam showed his work in New York at the Pierre Matisse Gallery, and in 1945 the Museum of Modern Art bought The Jungle; it has belonged to the museum ever since. One of Lam's most famous paintings, it is Surrealism made from the Caribbean side of the Atlantic, with the plantation and the faith of [[regla-de-ocha|Santería]] at its heart.
