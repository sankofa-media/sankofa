Afro-Mexicans descend largely from Africans brought to New Spain in colonial times for forced labour on farms and sugar plantations, the world of [[yanga|Gaspar Yanga]]. Yet modern Mexico barely acknowledged them, as if they were no part of the nation at all, and until 2015 no nationwide official count of them existed.

That changed through a long campaign by Afro-Mexican organisations, built from the bottom up over some twenty years. In 2015 the national statistics office added a question on African descent to its Intercensal Survey, the first time a nationally representative survey had asked it. 1,381,853 people identified themselves as Afro-descendant, about 1.2 percent of the population.

Four years later the law caught up. On 9 August 2019 a decree added a new section C to Article 2 of the Constitution: "This Constitution recognises the Afro-Mexican peoples and communities, whatever they call themselves, as part of the pluricultural composition of the Nation."

Being counted mattered. Numbers made a population that official Mexico had overlooked visible, and gave it a basis for policies of its own.
