Tsotsitaal is the street speech of South Africa's townships. Linguists call it a style rather than a separate language: a fast-changing slang vocabulary laid over an everyday local language, spoken mostly by young urban men as a sign of being streetwise. It first took shape in the 1940s in the mixed townships of Johannesburg, above all Sophiatown, where people of many languages lived side by side, and its earliest base was a non-standard Afrikaans.

Much of the vocabulary was borrowed from Afrikaans, the language of the white bosses. But on the streets of Sophiatown young speakers twisted the meaning and the order of the words, and took more from the American gangster films that were popular there. The point was to shut outsiders out, and it worked: in the 1940s hardly any white person understood it.

In the 1950s the government cleared Sophiatown under the Group Areas Act and moved its people to segregated townships such as Soweto. The street speech moved with them. Their children built tsotsitaal on urban Zulu or Sotho instead of Afrikaans, and today versions of it, under names such as iscamtho, are heard in townships across the country. In 2003 the scholar Louis Molamu published a dictionary of the Sophiatown words.

Tsotsitaal shows young people in a segregated city taking the language of power apart and rebuilding it as a sign of their own urban identity.
