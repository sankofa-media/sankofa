Tsotsitaal is the street slang of South Africa's townships, an argot spoken above all by young men, for whom it is the sign of being streetwise. Its name means "tsotsi language": the tsotsis were the young gangsters of Johannesburg. It took shape in the 1940s in the city's mixed townships, above all Sophiatown, where people of many languages lived side by side, and its first base was a non-standard Afrikaans.

Much of the vocabulary came from Afrikaans, the language of the white bosses, mixed with English. On the streets of Sophiatown young speakers kept reshaping the words and inventing new ones, and took lines from the American gangster films that Sophiatown loved. The point was to shut outsiders out.

In the 1950s the government began clearing Sophiatown under the Group Areas Act and moved its people to segregated townships such as Soweto. The street speech moved with them. Their children built new versions on urban Zulu or Sotho instead of Afrikaans, among them iscamtho, and today tsotsitaal in its local forms is heard in townships across the country. In 2003 the scholar Louis Molamu published a dictionary of the Sophiatown words.

Tsotsitaal shows young people in a segregated city taking the language of power apart and rebuilding it as a sign of their own urban identity.
