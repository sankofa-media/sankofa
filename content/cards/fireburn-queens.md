Denmark abolished slavery in its West Indian colony in 1848, but a labour law of 1849 kept the freed workers of St Croix on the plantations: a fixed day wage and the right to change employer only once a year, on 1 October. On that day in 1878 workers crowded into Frederiksted. Police beat a labourer, Henry Trotman; word spread that he had died, and the crowd turned on the fort, and by nightfall the town was burning. Over the following days fire took around fifty plantations and most of Frederiksted, and the revolt became the Fireburn, the largest labour uprising in Danish colonial history. It had no single command, but among its leaders were women remembered as queens: Mary Thomas, Queen Mary; Axeline Salomon, Queen Agnes; Mathilda McBean; and Susanna Abrahamson. Soldiers and militia crushed the revolt. The women were convicted and served part of their sentences in a women's prison in Copenhagen. In March 2018 the artists Jeannette Ehlers and La Vaughn Belle unveiled I Am Queen Mary outside Copenhagen's old West India Warehouse: a seven-metre figure on a wicker throne, holding a torch and a cane knife, on a plinth of coral stone cut by enslaved Africans on St Croix. It was the first public monument to a Black woman in Denmark.
