Puerto Limón is Costa Rica's port on the Caribbean. In the 1870s the government set out to build a railway from the coffee-growing Central Valley down to Limón, and to do it, it brought in workers from Jamaica. The line reached San José only in 1890. Many workers stayed, and the banana plantations that the United Fruit Company spread along the tracks drew more West Indians still.

Limón became an English-speaking world inside a Spanish-speaking country. Its people lived their lives in English, went to Protestant churches and joined the Universal Negro Improvement Association. Most were not Costa Rican citizens. In 1934, when disease sent United Fruit to new plantations on the Pacific coast, the banana contract signed with the government barred "people of colour" from working for the company there. Black Costa Ricans born in the country argued that the ban could not apply to citizens, and lost in court.

The ban fell in 1949. A popular story adds that a law also stopped Black people from travelling inland from Limón; historians have found no such law, only everyday discrimination and the ban on Pacific jobs. Limón's mix of peoples, languages and music still sets it apart from every other city in Costa Rica.
