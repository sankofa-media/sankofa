Mary Ann Shadd was born free in 1823 in Wilmington, Delaware, a slave state, the eldest child of an abolitionist family whose home sheltered people escaping slavery. In 1850 the Fugitive Slave Act let slave-catchers reach into the free North, and Black Americans began to leave for Canada. In 1851 Shadd followed them to Windsor, in Canada West, and opened a school for the children of refugees.

In 1852 she published A Plea for Emigration, urging Black Americans to settle in Canada. In March 1853 the first issue of the Provincial Freeman appeared. The abolitionist Samuel Ringgold Ward lent his name as editor, since a woman's name on the masthead could cost readers; Shadd was the driving force and spent a year on lecture tours raising subscriptions. From 1854 the paper came out weekly in Toronto under the motto "Self-reliance is the true road to independence", later moving to Chatham, until it folded. She was the first Black woman in North America to publish and edit a newspaper.

During the Civil War she recruited Black soldiers for the Union army. She then taught in Washington, studied law at Howard University and became one of the first Black women in the United States to practise it. She died in Washington in 1893.
