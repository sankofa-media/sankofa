Spirituals are the religious folk songs that enslaved African Americans made in the South, most likely from the late eighteenth century, out of Bible stories, hymns and African ways of singing. A leader improvised a line and the others answered, the call and answer of [[work-songs|work songs]]. In the ring shout, worshippers shuffled round in a circle. Songs of the Hebrews in Egypt and of Canaan spoke of the singers' own bondage and hope of freedom.

{male} [[douglass-covey|Frederick Douglass]], born enslaved in Maryland, heard as a boy the songs of people walking to the Great House Farm of the Lloyd plantation, and missed their meaning: "I was myself within the circle." In 1836 he and his friends plotted to run away, singing of Canaan: "We meant to reach the north, and the north was our Canaan." The plot failed; in 1838 he escaped. In the North he was astonished to hear the singing called contentment. "Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy," his Narrative of 1845 replied; every tone was "a testimony against slavery". Songs as sung escape maps came later: "Follow the Drinking Gourd" was first printed in 1928, one of [[underground-railroad-myths|the myths of the Underground Railroad]].

{female} Ella Sheppard was born enslaved in 1851. In despair her mother ran with her to the river to drown them both; an old woman stopped her. Her father bought Ella for $350; her mother stayed enslaved in Mississippi, and Ella found her only years later. A pianist trained in Cincinnati, Sheppard became Fisk's first Black teacher, and on 6 October 1871 she set out with students who would sing the school out of debt. "We did not dream of ever using them in public," she recalled of the spirituals: they "were associated with slavery and the dark past". Audiences loved them most, and Sheppard brought in more, among them "[[swing-low|Swing Low, Sweet Chariot]]", whose chorus one account traces to that old woman.

In 1873 the Fisk Jubilee Singers from Nashville sang spirituals for Queen Victoria; their tours paid for Jubilee Hall, still standing on the campus. Then came the concert hall of [[burleigh-dvorak|Harry T. Burleigh]], the [[blues|blues]] and [[gospel|gospel]].
