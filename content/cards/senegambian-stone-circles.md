Along the Gambia River, in what are now Senegal and The Gambia, stand more than a thousand megalithic monuments: rings of upright stones set beside burial mounds, spread along some 350 kilometres of the river. Excavations date the sites from about the third century BCE to the sixteenth century CE, so people kept raising them for more than fifteen hundred years.

The builders cut the pillars from nearby quarries of laterite with iron tools and stood them upright, each pillar a single block of stone. Most are cylinders or many-sided columns, laid out in remarkably regular rings; a few are split into a V, the so-called lyre stones. The monuments mark burials, and their scale and regularity point to a prosperous, well-organised society able to quarry, move and raise stone for centuries.

The largest group is at Sine Ngayène in Senegal. At Wassu, in The Gambia, eleven circles stand with frontal stones set before them.

In 2006 four groups, Sine Ngayène and Wanar in Senegal and Wassu and Kerbatch in The Gambia, with 93 circles between them, were inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Some pillars have fallen, but more than four centuries after the last of them was raised, many still stand where their builders set them.
