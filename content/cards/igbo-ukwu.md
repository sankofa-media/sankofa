Igbo-Ukwu is a town in the lower Niger region of southeastern Nigeria. Around the 9th century CE a wealthy society here, ancestors of today's Igbo, cast bronzes of startling refinement: ritual vessels, pendants, a crown, staff ornaments, all covered in fine decoration. Made by the lost-wax method in leaded bronze, they are the earliest artistic castings known from sub-Saharan Africa.

In 1938 Isaiah Anozie was digging a water cistern in his compound when he struck dozens of cast-metal objects. In 1959–60 the archaeologist Thurstan Shaw excavated the family land and named three sites after the Anozie brothers: Igbo Isaiah, Igbo Richard and Igbo Jonah. Igbo Isaiah held a store of ritual bronzes; Igbo Richard, the burial of a person of high rank, dressed in beaded wristlets and anklets, a pectoral and a copper crown. In all, the sites gave up more than 100,000 glass and carnelian beads.

Shaw's radiocarbon dates pointed to the 9th century, and the debate about the age of the finds and the origin of their glass and metal has run ever since.

Igbo-Ukwu's smiths were working centuries before the casters of [[benin-bronzes|Benin]] and of [[ife-heads|Ife]], and in a style entirely their own.
