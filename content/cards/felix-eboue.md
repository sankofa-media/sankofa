In June 1940 France fell to Germany, and the governors of its African colonies had to choose between the armistice and Charles de Gaulle's call to fight on. In Chad the governor was Félix Éboué, a Black colonial administrator born in Cayenne, French Guiana, in 1884. He had spent some twenty years in Ubangi-Shari, today's Central African Republic, where he learned local languages and wrote books about them and their peoples. In 1936 he became governor of Guadeloupe, and in 1938 he was moved to Chad.

On 29 June 1940 he cabled his superior that Chad would stay in the war, and he made contact with de Gaulle. On 26 August, at Fort-Lamy, today's N'Djamena, he proclaimed that Chad was joining Free France. Cameroon, Congo and Ubangi-Shari soon followed. In November de Gaulle made Éboué governor-general of all French Equatorial Africa, and in early 1944 he opened his conference on the future of France's African territories in Éboué's capital, Brazzaville.

Éboué died in Cairo on 17 May 1944. In May 1949 his remains were placed in the Panthéon in Paris, among the heroes of France.
