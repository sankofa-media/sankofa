Cowries are small, glossy sea-snail shells that served as money in much of West Africa for centuries, and in the Atlantic slave trade they became a price paid for human beings. The money cowrie came mostly from the Maldives. Islanders laid coconut-leaf mats on the water, let the molluscs settle, then dried them on the beach until only shells remained; sailors took them to the great market of Bengal. Europeans bought cowries mainly for West Africa, where trading partners preferred them to other payment. Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries ships carried them by the million to the Bight of Benin, the coast of [[aro-and-dahomey|the kingdom of Dahomey]], and cowries were paid directly for captives. The flood had a cost of its own: so many shells arrived that the cowrie lost much of its value. In the colonial era governments pushed coins and tried to ban the shells, yet cowries were still used for small purchases into the twentieth century. The shell tied Indian Ocean islands, Bengal, European ships and West African markets into one chain of the trade that [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]] tracks voyage by voyage, a very different African fortune from the gold of [[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]].
