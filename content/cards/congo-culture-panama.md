In the Spanish colonial era Portobelo, founded in 1597 on Panama's Caribbean coast, was one of the empire's principal ports, a nexus of the trade between the Americas and Spain. Enslaved Africans who escaped into the forests and mountains, the cimarrones, built fortified villages, palenques, and fought for their freedom. Their descendants and heirs, the Congos, tell that history each year in Carnival. During the Congo season the community gathers around a queen and her court. Against them stand the diablos, the devils, who represent the Spanish enslavers and carry whips. The climax comes on Ash Wednesday. The devils chase and lash at people; the Congos catch them and baptise them. Last of all the chief devil is captured, baptised and symbolically sold, the enslaver now treated as his captives once were. Then Carnival ends and Lent begins. In 2018 UNESCO inscribed the tradition on its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. Scholars read it as a living history: the story of self-liberation, told with parody, music and dance by the people whose ancestors lived it.
