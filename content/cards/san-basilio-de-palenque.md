In the seventeenth century Cartagena de Indias was the main port through which enslaved Africans entered Spanish South America, and people who escaped from its estates, mines and households built fortified villages in the hills. They were called palenques, after the wooden palisades around them. San Basilio, in the foothills of the Montes de María south-east of Cartagena, is the only one that has survived. Historians trace it to the palenques of the Sierra de María that outlasted the Spanish campaigns against the maroons at the end of the 1600s. In December 1713 the bishop of Cartagena, Antonio María Cassiani, met its people; under the agreement that followed they were pardoned, and the settlement, until then San Miguel Arcángel, became San Basilio. Long isolation let a culture of its own grow there. Palenqueros speak their own language, [[palenquero|Palenquero]], the only creole in Latin America built on Spanish words and the grammar of Bantu languages. Life is organised in ma kuagro, age groups that share work and duties, and death is marked with funeral rites of their own. The village names [[benkos-bioho|Benkos Biohó]] as its founder, although the documents do not prove the link. In 2008 UNESCO inscribed its cultural space on the list of the intangible heritage of humanity.
