In 1960, as one African colony after another became independent, the Soviet Union competed with the West for the loyalty of the new states. Education was one of its weapons. On 5 February 1960 the Soviet government decided to found a new university in Moscow to train specialists for the countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America: the Peoples' Friendship University. In February 1961, after the murder of the Congolese prime minister Patrice Lumumba, it was given his name.

African students arrived full of hope, and many found more than they had bargained for. They complained of racist insults on the street, of limits on travel and on their own student associations, and of trouble whenever they dated Soviet women.

In December 1963 a Ghanaian student was found dead, and his fellow students suspected he had been murdered. Days later African students marched on the Kremlin and Red Square, in the first unauthorised political demonstration Moscow had seen since the late 1920s. Yet African students kept coming.

The university trained generations of graduates for the Global South. In 1992 it was renamed the Peoples' Friendship University of Russia.
