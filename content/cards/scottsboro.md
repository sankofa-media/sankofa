In the Great Depression young Americans with no money rode freight trains in search of work. On 25 March 1931 a fight broke out between Black and white youths on a freight crossing Jackson County, Alabama. The train was stopped at Paint Rock, and nine Black youths, the youngest 13, were arrested. Two white women found aboard, Victoria Price and Ruby Bates, fearing charges of their own, accused them of rape.

In Scottsboro, the county seat, the National Guard was called in to protect the jail. Within days all-white juries convicted the nine, and eight were sentenced to death. The International Labor Defense, a radical legal group, took over their defence and won a stay of the executions. The Supreme Court overturned the convictions twice: in 1932 because the defendants had been denied proper counsel, and in 1935 because Black citizens had been kept off the juries. In 1933 Ruby Bates took back her story in court. Alabama kept trying the men anyway.

None was executed. In 1937 the state dropped the charges against four of them; the others spent years in prison. Clarence Norris, the last survivor, was pardoned in 1976. In 2013 Alabama passed a law to clear the nine, and in November its parole board pardoned the last three.
