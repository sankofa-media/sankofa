In eighteenth-century France a maxim older than the colonies still had force: no one is a slave in France, and an enslaved person who touches French soil becomes free. Lawyers traced it to an edict of Louis X in 1315. Meanwhile France's Caribbean colonies ran on slave labour, and planters returning home brought enslaved servants with them.

The crown tried to protect those masters. An edict of 1716 let them keep slaves in France if they completed formalities; a declaration of 1738 tightened the rules, ordering that slaves of masters who skipped them be confiscated for the king rather than freed. Still, men and women held in Paris went to the Admiralty Court of France and sued for their freedom on the old maxim, and many of them won.

In 1777 Louis XVI changed the question from slavery to colour. His Declaration for the police des Noirs barred "Blacks, mulattoes and other people of colour" from entering the kingdom. A colonist could still bring one Black servant for the voyage, but on landing the servant was to be held in a depot in the port until shipped back. France's proud maxim of freedom and its laws of exclusion by colour grew up side by side.
