Osmanya is an alphabet made for Somali, a language whose poetry and history had long been passed on by word of mouth. Religious scholars had written Somali in Arabic letters, and others tried the Latin alphabet. Osmanya was neither: an original script of its own, made for Somali.

Its inventor, Cismaan Yuusuf Keenadiid, known in English as Osman Yusuf Kenadid, devised it around 1920. When Somalia became independent, the question of how to write the national language turned into a deadlock. Supporters of Latin, of Arabic and of Osmanya would not yield, and commissions studied the question for years without a decision.

After the 1969 coup the new military government made the script question a priority. On 21 October 1972 it announced a national orthography in Latin letters and made Somali the official language of the state. A mass literacy campaign followed in 1973.

Osmanya lost, but it did not vanish. In 2003 it was encoded in Unicode 4.0, so its letters can still be typed and read today. Like [[adlam|Adlam]] or [[vai-script|Vai]], it is one of the scripts Africans made for their own languages.
