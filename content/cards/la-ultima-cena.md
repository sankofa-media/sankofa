The Last Supper, made in Cuba in 1976 by Tomás Gutiérrez Alea, is set on a sugar plantation near Havana at the end of the eighteenth century, when the island's sugar was worked by enslaved Africans. Its idea came from the Cuban historian Manuel Moreno Fraginals and his economic study El ingenio, The Sugar Mill, of 1964. There he told of the Count of Casa Bayona, who, to ease his conscience, washed the feet of twelve of the men he enslaved on Holy Thursday and invited them to dine with him.

In the film the count plays Christ. He washes and kisses the feet of twelve bewildered men, then holds forth at the table for almost an hour of screen time. The men take his kindness at its word. On Good Friday they rebel and seize the overseer, and the count, master once again, answers with brutal repression.

Alea turned a pious gesture into an allegory of the religious hypocrisy of a slave society. Restored in recent years, the film is again being shown in cinemas and at festivals in Europe.
