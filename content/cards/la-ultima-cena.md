The Last Supper, made in Cuba in 1976 by Tomás Gutiérrez Alea, is set during Holy Week on a sugar plantation near Havana at the end of the eighteenth century, when the island's sugar boom ran on the labour of enslaved Africans. The historian Manuel Moreno Fraginals advised on it, and its story starts from a real event: one Holy Thursday a Count of Casa Bayona washed the feet of twelve of the men he enslaved and sat down to eat with them.

In the film the count casts himself as Christ and twelve bewildered men as his apostles. At the table he holds forth for almost an hour of screen time, mixing Christian teaching with justifications for owning them. The men take his words seriously. On Good Friday they rise up, and the count, master once again, crushes the revolt and has his "apostles" beheaded.

Alea turned a pious gesture into an allegory of the religious hypocrisy of a slave society. Restored in recent years, the film is again being shown at festivals and in cinemas in Europe, from Venice to the Barbican in London.
