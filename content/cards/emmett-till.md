Emmett Till, fourteen, left Chicago in August 1955 to visit
family in the Mississippi Delta. On 24 August he stopped at Bryant's store
in Money. Four nights later Roy Bryant and J. W. Milam took him from his
great-uncle's house; his body was found in the Tallahatchie River.

{male} The great-uncle, Moses Wright, was a sixty-four-year-old
sharecropper and preacher. Milam threatened to kill him if he talked;
Wright went to the sheriff the same day. At the trial in Sumner, which
opened on 19 September, he stood up and pointed at Milam, then at Bryant.
In Mississippi a Black man almost never accused white men in court.

{female} His mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, wanted the world to see.
The sheriff hurried to bury the body; she brought it home to Chicago,
held an open-casket funeral at Roberts Temple and let Jet photographer
David Jackson take pictures, printed in September. At the trial, which opened
in Sumner on 19 September, she testified that the body was her son's,
against claims that it was not.

An all-white jury acquitted both men in about an hour. In January 1956,
safe from retrial, they sold their confession to Look.

{male} Soon after the verdict Wright moved his family north, to
Chicago. Six decades after [[ida-b-wells|Ida B. Wells]] gathered the
figures on lynching, a Black farmer had named the killers in the court
that freed them.

{female} His mother spoke against injustice across the country until
she died in 2003. Six decades after [[ida-b-wells|Ida B. Wells]] gathered
the figures on lynching, a mother made the country look at one.

In December 1955 Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat;
she later said she had thought of Emmett Till. In 2022 a law in his
name made lynching a federal hate crime, and in 2023 the church,
the courthouse and the river landing became a national monument to Emmett
and his mother.
