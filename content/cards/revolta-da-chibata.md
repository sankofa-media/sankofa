In 1910, two decades after abolition, the lash was still a punishment in Brazil's navy. The sailors were almost all Black or of mixed race, the officers white. On the night of 22 November 1910, in Guanabara Bay, sailors led by João Cândido took over the battleship Minas Gerais, killing several officers including her captain, and the São Paulo and other ships joined them. The rebels trained their guns on Rio de Janeiro and sent President Hermes da Fonseca an ultimatum: abolish the lash, or the capital would be shelled.

Congress passed the amnesty, flogging was abolished, and at the end of November the sailors handed back the ships. Within days, though, sailors were being expelled; in December a new revolt on Ilha das Cobras was crushed by bombardment. Hundreds of sailors were jailed; some died in their cells, some were shot, others were sent to Amazon rubber forests. Cândido survived prison and a spell in an asylum, was thrown out of the navy and sold fish in Rio until his death in 1969, aged 89.

The press had called him the "Black Admiral"; in the 1970s censors made João Bosco and Aldir Blanc change it to "Black navigator" in their song about him. In 2008 a law granted him and his comrades a posthumous amnesty.
