Lélia Gonzalez was a Brazilian anthropologist and activist, born in Belo Horizonte in 1935, the second-youngest of eighteen children of a Black railway worker and a domestic worker of Indigenous descent. Brazil liked to call itself a "racial democracy", and she spent her life taking that myth apart.

The family moved to Rio de Janeiro when she was seven. She trained in history and philosophy, taught in schools and later at the Pontifical Catholic University of Rio. When the Unified Black Movement was launched in 1978, she was among the activists at its origin, and inside it she spoke up for Black women.

She gave her ideas names. Pretuguês was her word for the Portuguese of Brazil, Africanised in rhythm and sound: the "r" in "Framengo", for which Black speakers were mocked, she traced to African languages that lack an "l". In a 1988 essay she proposed Amefricanity, the Black experience shared across the whole continent and visible in its revolts and free settlements, from Brazil's quilombos to the palenques and maroon communities elsewhere in the Americas.

She died in 1994. In 2019 Angela Davis, visiting São Paulo, asked Brazilians why they looked to the United States for a guide: she learned more from Lélia Gonzalez, she said, than they could learn from her.
