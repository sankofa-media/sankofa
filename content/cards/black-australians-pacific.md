In January 1788 the First Fleet arrived from Britain to found a penal colony at Sydney Cove. Among its convicts were at least ten people of African descent, [[john-caesar|John Caesar]], later called Australia's first bushranger, among them. Most had come from America: they were among the enslaved people who ran to the British during the American Revolution, and after the war many ended up in England, where they were convicted and sentenced to transportation.

So was John Randall, a man from the American colonies convicted of theft in England in 1785. At Port Jackson he became one of the three convicts licensed to shoot game for the hungry settlement. Later he joined the New South Wales Corps, the colony's garrison, which wanted him for its band: he played the flute and the drum.

On 26 January 1808 the officers of the Corps deposed Governor William Bligh. Four hundred soldiers marched to Government House behind the regimental band, with Randall in it, and put the governor under arrest.

For a long time Australia's founding story left these men out. Historians, Mollie Gillen in 1989 and Cassandra Pybus in 2006 among them, have since recovered their names.
