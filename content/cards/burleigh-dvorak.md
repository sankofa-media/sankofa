Harry T. Burleigh, a young Black baritone from Erie, Pennsylvania, came to New York in 1892 on a scholarship to the National Conservatory of Music. Its new head was the Czech composer Antonín Dvořák, hired to help American composers find their own voice. Burleigh became Dvořák's assistant and the conservatory's librarian and copyist, and he sang the old plantation songs, the spirituals, for Dvořák. Dvořák told the press that "the future music of this country must be founded upon what are called Negro melodies." His Symphony No. 9, "From the New World", premiered at Carnegie Hall in December 1893 and was an immediate success. Burleigh said Dvořák quoted "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot" in it, though how directly the music borrows from spirituals is still debated.

Burleigh went on to sing as soloist at St George's Episcopal Church for more than fifty years and in 1914 helped found the American Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers (ASCAP). He also did something new: he set spirituals for voice and piano like European art songs, above all "Deep River" (1916–17), and carried the [[spirituals|spirituals]] into concert halls. He died in 1949. Before him, [[bridgetower|George Bridgetower]] had made his name in Europe's classical music; in churches the songs fed [[gospel|gospel]].
