Osogbo, the capital of Nigeria's Osun State, lies on the Osun River. On its edge stands a dense forest, one of the last patches of primary high forest in southern Nigeria, held to be the home of Osun, the Yoruba river goddess. Yoruba towns once kept such groves outside their walls; this is the largest to survive. The town's founding story binds its first ruler, Laro, to the goddess and to the fish of her river.

By the 1950s the grove's shrines were neglected and crumbling. Susanne Wenger, an Austrian artist who had come to Nigeria with her husband Ulli Beier, was initiated as a Yoruba priestess and set to work. With local craftsmen who grew into artists, the New Sacred Art movement, she rebuilt old shrines and raised giant new ones where the forest was most threatened.

The art staked out the grove against the spreading town. Part of it became a national monument in 1965, and in 2005 the grove became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Wenger died in Osogbo in 2009.

Every August Osogbo holds its festival for the goddess, with offerings to Osun at the river, and worship goes on in the grove week by week. Wenger's sculptures stand in a living sanctuary, not a museum.
