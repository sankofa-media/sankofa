The Brookes print is a diagram of a slave ship packed with people, made by British abolitionists to show the public what the Atlantic slave trade looked like inside a hull. In 1788 they commissioned a drawing of how people were stowed aboard the Liverpool slave ship Brooks, also spelled Brookes. The first version came out of Plymouth. In London James Phillips printed it in 1789 as Description of a Slave Ship, and some 7,000 copies with woodcuts were printed and given out. The plan shows 482 people laid in rows like cargo; the real ship had often carried more than 600. The image travelled across the country and abroad, and a cargo list became a picture nobody could unsee.

The campaign it served won its first goal eighteen years later. On 25 March 1807 the Act for the Abolition of the Slave Trade became law, banning the trade in enslaved people across the British Empire from 1 May that year. The Act ended the trade, not slavery itself. The print's partner in the campaign was [[wedgwood-medallion|the Wedgwood medallion "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?"]]; the reality behind both is recorded in [[zong|the Zong massacre]] and in the voyages of [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]].
