In Paris between the world wars, students from the French Caribbean and Africa met Black American writers of the Harlem Renaissance, and one place they met was a salon. Paulette Nardal and her sisters, from Martinique, hosted it in Clamart, just outside Paris. Léopold Sédar Senghor and Aimé Césaire came, and so did Claude McKay. In 1931 Paulette and the Haitian Léo Sajous launched La Revue du monde noir, bilingual in French and English. It lasted six issues in about six months: a short run, and a beginning. Césaire, Senghor and Léon-Gontran Damas went on to found Négritude, and Césaire coined its name; the women who prepared the ground were long overlooked. In 1939, sailing back to Paris after a stay at home, Paulette was badly injured when a German submarine torpedoed her ship, and she settled in Martinique for good. There she founded the Rassemblement féminin and a journal for women voting for the first time. She died in 1985.
