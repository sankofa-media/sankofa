The Siddi are Indians of African descent. For centuries they were called Habshi, from the Arabic for Abyssinian; today most live in Karnataka and Gujarat, with smaller groups in Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere. Many of their ancestors reached India enslaved, carried across the Indian Ocean by traders. In India's kingdoms, though, enslaved Africans could rise: some became generals, ministers and kingmakers.

The first famous name is Jamal ud-Din Yaqut, an Abyssinian who rose high at the court of Raziya, the woman sultan of Delhi, in the 1230s; legend later made them lovers, though historians doubt it. Two centuries on, a king of Bengal recruited some 8,000 Abyssinian soldiers, and in the late 1480s Abyssinian nobles seized the throne itself. Their Habshi dynasty ruled Bengal until 1493.

On the west coast, African rule lasted into modern times. In 1791 a Siddi prince received a small state of his own, Sachin, and his family ruled it until 1948, when it joined independent India.

Siddi history runs from the Delhi throne to today's villages, a story of Africans who shaped India from the inside.
