The Gombeys are Bermuda's masked street dancers. A troupe moves through the streets behind its drummers, the dancers in bright costumes set with mirrors and in masks that hide every face. "Gombey" is a Bantu word for rhythm, and the tradition mixes West African, British, Caribbean and Native American elements.

It grew among Bermuda's enslaved people, with roots in Africa. The dances gave them a way to act out and comment on their own condition, and the masks kept the dancers anonymous. For a long time the Gombeys came out only at the year's end holidays, the few days the enslaved were not forced to work, and the island's rulers looked on them with suspicion.

The masks never came off, but the outlook changed. Troupes passed from father to son, each family keeping its own style, and in recent decades the Gombeys have gone from a tradition the island's elite disdained to a symbol of Bermuda itself, out on the streets for its celebrations.

They are a living link to what Bermuda's enslaved people brought with them, kept alive by the families who still dance it.
