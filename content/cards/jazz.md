Jazz is dance music made up on the spot. It emerged in New Orleans around 1900, mainly among Black and Creole musicians, out of ragtime, the [[blues|blues]], [[spirituals|spirituals]] and the city's brass-band marches, played with a ragged, swinging rhythm. A typical band had a front line of cornet, clarinet and trombone over a rhythm section; many players did not read music and worked numbers out by ear. They improvised together, each horn weaving its own line around the tune, for dances, picnics, parades and brass-band funerals. The roots ran deeper: on Sunday afternoons enslaved Africans had gathered in New Orleans' Congo Square to drum, sing and dance.

The cornetist Buddy Bolden, pictured here, was its first king; in 1907 he was committed to the state asylum at Jackson, where he died in 1931, and no recording of him is known to survive. The first jazz record, "Livery Stable Blues" (Victor, 1917), was cut instead by the white Original Dixieland Jass Band from New Orleans. It was an unexpected hit, and the band claimed to have invented jazz rather than credit their Black teachers.

[[crazy-blues|Mamie Smith's "Crazy Blues"]] showed in 1920 that Black artists' records would sell. Louis Armstrong came north to Chicago in 1922, when King Oliver sent for him, to the city whose [[chicago-defender|Chicago Defender]] urged Black Southerners north, and from 1925 his Hot Five and Hot Seven turned jazz into a soloist's art.
