Jazz is dance music made up on the spot. It emerged in New Orleans around 1900, mainly among Black and Creole musicians, out of ragtime, the [[blues|blues]], [[spirituals|spirituals]] and brass-band marches, played with a ragged, swinging rhythm. A front line of cornet, clarinet and trombone improvised together, each horn on its own line, over a rhythm section, for dances, parades and funerals. Its roots ran back to Congo Square, where enslaved Africans had gathered on Sundays to drum, sing and dance.

Its first king, the cornetist Buddy Bolden, pictured here, was committed to an asylum in 1907 and left no known recording. The first jazz record, "Livery Stable Blues" (1917), was cut instead by the white Original Dixieland Jass Band, which claimed to have invented jazz rather than credit its Black teachers. In 1920 [[crazy-blues|Mamie Smith's "Crazy Blues"]] showed that Black artists' records would sell, while the [[chicago-defender|Chicago Defender]] urged Black Southerners north.

{male} In New Orleans the young Louis Armstrong took lessons from the city's top cornetist, King Oliver. In 1922 Oliver sent for him to join his band in Chicago. After a year in New York with Fletcher Henderson, Armstrong returned in 1925 to record under his own name. His Hot Five and Hot Seven turned jazz from an ensemble music into a soloist's art.

{female} Lil Hardin, a Memphis pianist trained at Fisk University, joined King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band in Chicago in 1921. In 1924 she married its second cornetist, Louis Armstrong, and persuaded him to leave Oliver for New York, as Fletcher Henderson's star soloist. A year later he was back, making the Hot Five records, which turned jazz into a soloist's art; she played piano and wrote tunes such as "Struttin' with Some Barbecue". She died in 1971 at a concert in his memory.
