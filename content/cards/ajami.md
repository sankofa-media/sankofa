Ajami is the name for African languages written in a modified Arabic script, with the letters adapted to render their sounds. For centuries people across West and East Africa have used it to write about a dozen languages, among them Wolof, Hausa, Mandinka and Swahili, and not only scholars: poets wrote verse in it, and ordinary people used it for business records and daily notes. The oldest firmly dated Swahili writing on paper, letters from [[swahili|Kilwa]] in 1711, is Ajami.

The linguist Fallou Ngom grew up in Senegal and was teaching French linguistics at Western Washington University when, years after his father's death, he came upon a note in which his father had recorded a debt. His father had been considered illiterate because he could not read French, Senegal's official language. Yet the note was written in his own language, in Arabic letters.

Ngom turned his research to Ajami. At Boston University he built the African Ajami Library, and his team, working with the British Library's Endangered Archives Programme, digitised thousands of pages of Mandinka Ajami manuscripts from private collections in Casamance, southern Senegal: religious poems, prayers, divination manuals, personal letters.

By official standards such writers counted as illiterate. Their own pages show communities that wrote down their faith, business and family lives in their own languages.
