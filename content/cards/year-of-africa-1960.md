In 1960 seventeen colonies in Africa became independent, so many that the year is remembered as the Year of Africa. Among them was the Belgian Congo. In January and February 1960 Congolese politicians met Belgian officials at a Round Table in the Palais des Congrès in Brussels, and the conference set independence for 30 June.

The delegation took its music along. The brothers Thomas and Philippe Kanza brought a band to Brussels: the singer Joseph Kabasele, known as Le Grand Kallé, with musicians from his African Jazz and from the rival OK Jazz. In Brussels the band introduced Kabasele's "Indépendance Cha Cha", a Congolese rumba in cha-cha time, sung in Lingala and French, that told the conference as it happened and named the parties and their leaders one after another: Kasavubu, Lumumba, Tshombe and the rest, as if all of them were on one side. It became a hit far beyond the Congo, and other African countries took it up as their own independence anthem.

The unity in the song did not last. Soon after independence Katanga, under Moïse Tshombe, declared its secession; Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba was ousted, imprisoned and, early in 1961, killed in Katanga. The song outlived it: it is perhaps the most famous Congolese song, the sound of a year when a continent changed its map.
