In 1739 South Carolina was a British colony with an enslaved Black majority, and Spanish Florida promised freedom to those who reached it. On Sunday 9 September, near the Stono River south of Charleston, enslaved men, several of them from Kongo, broke into a store, took guns and marched south, shouting "Liberty!". They killed at least twenty white colonists, and their numbers grew to perhaps a hundred; some accounts say they beat drums as they marched. That afternoon the militia caught up with them in a field and routed them. Rebels were killed on the spot, and most of those who escaped were hunted down. It was the colony's largest slave rising, and the colony answered by going after the drums. In 1740 South Carolina passed the Negro Act, which restricted enslaved people's drums and stopped owners from freeing slaves at will. Dance historians argue that the rhythm then moved into the body: patting juba, hambone, and later tap and stepping. Other colonies tried the same: in Trinidad a ban on drumming led, in time, to the [[steelpan|steelpan]].
