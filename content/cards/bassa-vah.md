Bassa Vah is an alphabet for Bassa, a tonal language of Liberia. It is written from left to right and has 23 consonants, 7 vowels and 5 tone marks. The marks sit inside the open space of each vowel letter, and they are never left off: a Bassa word written without its tone is misspelled.

Its modern form is owed to Thomas Flo Lewis, a Bassa from Liberia who studied in Syracuse, New York, at the start of the 20th century. From around 1907 he had type made and printed texts in the script, and back in Liberia he taught it to students. Where he learned it is a mystery. Bassa tradition says an older system of signs had lived on among Bassa people enslaved in the Americas, and that Lewis met their descendants on his travels; the documents to prove it have not been found.

Lewis died in 1935, and the books he printed are now rare fragments. But the alphabet outlived him, kept up by Bassa teachers and scholars in Liberia and abroad. In June 2014 Bassa Vah entered the Unicode Standard in version 7.0, and it now stands there beside other scripts that West Africans made for their own languages, among them [[vai-script|Vai]] and [[kikakui|Mende Kikakui]].
