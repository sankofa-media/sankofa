Loíza is a coastal town in north-eastern Puerto Rico and the island's great centre of African heritage. Every July it keeps the Fiestas of Santiago Apóstol, St James, the warrior saint of Spain who was said to have driven the Moors from Iberia.

Legend says a small carved image of the saint was found among the roots of an old cork tree at Las Carreras, outside the town. Today there are three images, St James of the Men, of the Women and of the Children, and on 26, 27 and 28 July each in turn is carried in procession through the streets.

Around the saints dance masked characters: Caballeros, knights in Spanish finery; Locas, men dressed as wild women; ragged Viejos, the old men; and the vejigantes, the devils of the feast. In Ponce, on the south coast, vejigante masks are papier-mâché. In Loíza they are carved from coconut husks and crowned with horns, and they are said to bring the ancestors back from the other world. Local carvers make them, among them Castor Ayala and, after him, his family.

In Loíza the crusading saint of Spain became the people's saint, and the devils who dance before him wear masks carved by the descendants of Africans.
