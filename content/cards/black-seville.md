In the sixteenth century Seville was one of the largest centres of slavery in Spain and a busy slave market. In the mid-sixteenth century enslaved people made up about 7.4 per cent of the inhabitants recorded in its census.

The city's Black people had an institution of their own long before that. Around 1393 Archbishop Gonzalo de Mena founded a hospital with a chapel, Los Ángeles, to shelter destitute Black people. Around it grew a brotherhood that for its first centuries was made up only of Black people, enslaved and freed. It was a place to pray, to be cared for and to belong in a city that bought and sold its members. Like Seville's other brotherhoods, it carried its sacred images through the streets in Holy Week.

In later centuries Seville's Black population dwindled, and the brotherhood opened to others. It did not disappear. The Hermandad de los Negritos keeps its chapel of Our Lady of the Angels, and every Holy Thursday its members still carry the Christ and the Virgin through Seville: more than six hundred years of a Black institution in the heart of a European city.
