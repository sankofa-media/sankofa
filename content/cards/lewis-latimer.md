In the early 1880s several companies, Thomas Edison's among them, were trying to make the incandescent lamp practical, and its weak point was the carbon filament, which burned out quickly. Lewis Howard Latimer was born in Chelsea, Massachusetts, on 4 September 1848, the son of formerly enslaved parents. At sixteen he joined the Union Navy. After the war, with no formal schooling, he taught himself mechanical drawing and became a draftsman, and he made the drawings for Alexander Graham Bell's application for the telephone patent. In 1880 he joined the United States Electric Lighting Company in New York, where the inventor Hiram Maxim was working on lamps. On 17 January 1882 he received a patent, assigned to that company, for a process of making the carbons: the fibre blanks were baked into carbon sealed inside cardboard envelopes. The result was a filament that was more durable and lasted longer, and bulbs that more people could afford. Later he went to work for Edison's company as a patent investigator and expert witness. He died on 11 December 1928. Longer-lasting filaments that could be made in quantity helped carry electric light into ordinary homes.
