In the early 1880s inventors were racing to make the incandescent electric lamp practical, but its glowing heart was a thread of carbon so delicate that many broke before they ever reached a bulb. Lewis Howard Latimer was born in Chelsea, Massachusetts, on 4 September 1848, the son of formerly enslaved parents. At sixteen he joined the Union Navy. After the war he found work at a Boston patent law firm and, with no formal schooling, taught himself mechanical drawing until he became a draftsman. He made the drawings for Alexander Graham Bell's application for the telephone patent. In New York he went to work for the United States Electric Lighting Company. On 17 January 1882 he received a patent, assigned to that company, for a process of making the carbons: baked the old way, many of the delicate filaments broke and warped in the furnace, and his method stopped that. The result was a filament that lasted longer, and bulbs that more people could afford. He later joined Thomas Edison's company as a patent expert. He died on 11 December 1928. His method had helped turn the electric bulb from a costly novelty into something ordinary households could afford.
