Samba is a music and a dance at once. Its root, samba de roda, still gathers people in a circle in the Recôncavo of Bahia: participants sing, play, clap and take turns dancing in the middle. It drew on the dances of the region's enslaved Africans.

Migrants carried samba de roda from Bahia to Rio, where Bahian "aunts" led the Black neighbourhood around Praça Onze, called Little Africa. From that world came Pelo Telefone, registered at the National Library in 1916. It was the hit of the 1917 carnival and is often called Brazil's first samba. Who made it, and where, is still argued over.

{female} The most famous aunt, Tia Ciata, was born Hilária Batista de Almeida in Bahia in 1854. She came to Rio with a daughter and sold Bahian sweets from a tray in the city centre. At the [[candomble|candomblé]] house of João Alabá she became mãe-pequena, second in rank. Her home on Rua Visconde de Itaúna held rites for the orishas and samba. Her husband, João Batista da Silva, was a public servant, and the police, who raided such gatherings, left hers alone. Regulars said Pelo Telefone grew there from shared improvised verses, and popular memory keeps her house as the cradle of samba.

{male} The name on the score was Donga's: Ernesto Joaquim Maria dos Santos, the son of a Bahian mother. On 27 November 1916 the National Library registered Pelo Telefone under his name, and a song of the yards had an author. Critics said he had taken motifs sung at Tia Ciata's house; he later shared the credit with Mauro de Almeida. In 1922 he spent six months in Paris with Pixinguinha's Oito Batutas. Part of the Brazilian press objected that a mostly Black band should not represent the country, and in Paris, as Les Batutas, they played the nightclubs, the Shéhérazade above all.

From such yards came the samba schools of Rio's Black neighbourhoods, which compete at carnival, and the music of Little Africa became a symbol of Brazil.
