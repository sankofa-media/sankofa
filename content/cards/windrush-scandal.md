Between 1948 and 1973 many Commonwealth citizens, most of them from the Caribbean, came to live in Britain. They are called the Windrush generation, after the ship that brought Caribbean migrants to Tilbury in 1948. The Immigration Act 1971, in force from 1973, confirmed that those already settled could stay for good, but they were given no document to show it, and the Home Office kept no record. Many children had travelled on their parents' passports.

In 2012 the Home Secretary, Theresa May, introduced a "hostile environment" for illegal migration: employers, landlords and health services had to demand proof of status. People who had lived in Britain for decades could not prove it: they lost jobs, were refused medical care and benefits, detained, removed or refused re-entry. Landing-card slips recording arrival dates had been destroyed in 2010; the Home Office said they were not reliable evidence of residence or status.

In April 2018 the government admitted the Windrush generation had been treated unfairly and set up a taskforce, later a compensation scheme. In March 2020 Wendy Williams's independent review found the scandal "foreseeable and avoidable". Stopping short of finding institutional racism, it saw in the Home Office "an institutional ignorance and thoughtlessness towards the issue of race". The Home Secretary, Priti Patel, told the Commons she was "truly sorry".
