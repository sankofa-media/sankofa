In Spanish Louisiana, New Orleans had a sizeable community of free people of colour. Across the colony and the Caribbean, free and enslaved women of African descent wore the tignon, a headwrap that enslaved women used to keep their hair up and clean at work. In 1786 the Spanish governor ordered women of colour, free or not, to cover their hair in public. The aim was to tie free women of colour, visibly, to the enslaved rather than to white women. The women obeyed the letter of the law and defeated its purpose. They wrapped their heads in costly, brightly coloured fabrics tied in intricate knots and pinned with feathers and jewels. The headwrap meant to mark them as inferior became a sign of beauty, wealth and creativity, and the fashion outlived Spanish rule. An 1837 portrait by François Fleischbein, now in the Historic New Orleans Collection, shows a woman of colour in a tignon. For years it was said to depict the artist's housekeeper, Betsy, yet no one knows who she was.
