In the 1890s the British South Africa Company of Cecil Rhodes occupied the Zimbabwe plateau, seizing land and cattle and imposing taxes. Among the Shona, the spirits of great ancestors spoke through mediums. Charwe, who lived in the Mazowe valley, was the medium of Nehanda, a mhondoro, or royal ancestral spirit.

When the central Shona rose against the Company in June 1896, Charwe became a leading figure of the resistance in the Mazowe valley, and she held out until her capture in 1897. In March 1898 she was tried in Salisbury, now Harare, for the murder of the Native Commissioner Henry Pollard, killed in the rising, and sentenced to death. Whether she was guilty is still debated: the historian David Beach doubted the fairness of her trial. She refused to convert to Christianity and was hanged in April 1898.

Her name outlived the rising. It inspired the guerrillas of the 1970s war that ended white-minority Rhodesia, and after independence in 1980 a major road and a hospital maternity wing in Harare were named after her. In May 2021 a bronze statue of her was unveiled at a central Harare crossroads. A spirit medium from a river valley became the best-known symbol of resistance in modern Zimbabwe.
