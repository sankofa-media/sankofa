The blues is a way of turning trouble into song. It grew up among African Americans in the American South at the end of the nineteenth century. A common verse form has three lines: the second repeats the first, and the third answers it with a rhyme, all over twelve bars and three chords. The voice leans on blue notes, such as flattened thirds and sevenths, and the guitar often answers a line like a second singer. Its words are about mistreatment by lovers, hard times and travel.

{male} Around 1903 W. C. Handy led an orchestra in Clarksdale, Mississippi, playing dances for white and Black audiences. Waiting for a train at Tutwiler, he heard a man press a knife to his guitar strings and sing "Goin' where the Southern cross' the Dog". Handy called it the weirdest music he had ever heard, and began to work such country songs into his own. In 1912 "The Memphis Blues" made his name across the country; "St. Louis Blues" became his most popular song. Because his published blues grew from the sounds of the Delta, Handy became known as the "Father of the Blues", the title of his 1941 autobiography.

{female} Gertrude Pridgett, a singer from Columbus, Georgia, later recalled that in 1902, touring Missouri with a tent show, she heard a young woman come to the tent and sing a sad song about a man who had left her. Gertrude learned it and made it her encore. As Ma Rainey she gathered such songs across the South and brought them onto the vaudeville stage. Mamie Smith's [[crazy-blues|"Crazy Blues"]] of 1920 opened the record market to Black women singers, and in December 1923 Paramount began recording Rainey as the "Mother of the Blues". The women of her songs answered mistreatment instead of waiting on a man. Bessie Smith, who had learned from her on tour, made her first records for Columbia that year.

The blues grew out of [[work-songs|work songs, field hollers]] and [[spirituals|spirituals]], and its forms and sounds passed on into [[jazz|jazz]], [[gospel|gospel]] and [[rock-n-roll|rock and roll]].
