Harriet Jacobs was born into slavery in Edenton, North Carolina, around 1813, and as a young woman was held by Dr. James Norcom, who for years pressed her with sexual threats. She had two children by another white man, the lawyer Samuel Sawyer. In 1835, escaping Norcom, she ran no further than her grandmother's house in Edenton and hid in a crawlspace under its roof. "The garret was only nine feet long, and seven wide. The highest part was three feet high," she wrote. For nearly seven years she lived there, unable to stand, watching her children in the street through a hole.

In 1842 she escaped by boat to the North, where she was reunited with her children. In 1852 her employer, Cornelia Willis, bought her freedom. In 1861 she published Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl under the name Linda Brent, telling openly what enslaved women suffered from the men who owned them. For generations many doubted that a formerly enslaved woman had written it herself; in the 1980s scholars established her authorship. During the Civil War she worked among Black refugees, and she died in 1897.

Her garret has become one of the most haunting images of what escape from slavery could cost.
