From the sixteenth century the sultanates of the Deccan, in central India, bought enslaved Africans from the Ethiopian highlands, known as Habshis, as soldiers and servants. Malik Ambar was one of them. Born Chapu in Ethiopia in 1548, he was sold at the Red Sea port of Mocha and taken to Baghdad, where a merchant educated him, brought him up a Muslim and named him Ambar.

In the Deccan he was bought by Chengiz Khan, himself a former Ethiopian slave and chief minister of the sultanate of Ahmadnagar. Freed after his master's death, Ambar raised his own troops. When the Mughal emperors pushed south around 1600, he became the leader of the resistance, with an army in which Marathas fought beside Africans. Ruling through sultans he set on the throne, he was the real power in Ahmadnagar, and he beat Mughal armies again and again. He built a new capital, Khadki, with waterworks to bring it water; it later became Aurangabad.

Emperor Jahangir called him "the black-fated". Around 1616–20 a painter of his court, probably Abu'l Hasan, showed the emperor shooting arrows at Ambar's severed head: a victory won only in paint, over a foe his armies kept failing to crush. The picture is now in the Chester Beatty in Dublin.
