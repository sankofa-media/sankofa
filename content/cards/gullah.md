Gullah, or Geechee, is a creole that grew from English and many African languages among enslaved West and Central Africans on the rice, Sea Island cotton and indigo plantations of coastal South Carolina and Georgia. Its words are mostly English, its grammar is not simply English, and it keeps African words. Outsiders long dismissed it as broken English.

In 1933 at Harris Neck, Georgia, the linguist Lorenzo Dow Turner recorded Amelia Dawley singing a five-line song in a language she did not know. It was Mende, from Sierra Leone.

{male} Turner first heard Gullah in the summer of 1929 at South Carolina State College. Earlier scholars heard only imperfect English; he heard words that were not English. He took island speakers by rowboat to the mainland, where there was power for his hundred-pound recorder. His Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect (1949) traced thousands of its words and names to African languages: slavery had not erased the memory of Africa. In 1990, following his recording, Joseph Opala and Cynthia Schmidt found Baindu Jabati in Senehun Ngola still singing it.

{female} The song had come down through women. Dawley learned it from her mother; the family sang it to amuse the children. Among the Mende it was a funeral song, and women preside over funerals. In 1990 Cynthia Schmidt, searching southern Sierra Leone with Joseph Opala and Tazieff Koroma, played the recording in Senehun Ngola, and Baindu Jabati sang along. Her grandmother, who taught her the song, foretold that it would one day bring her lost kin. Civil war followed: rebels razed the village and held Jabati captive.

In 1997 Dawley's daughter, Mary Moran, came to the village; it received her with jubilation, and the two women sang the song together, as the film The Language You Cry In (1998) shows. Gullah is still spoken and belongs with [[spirituals|the spirituals]] and [[work-songs|the work songs and field hollers]] among what the enslaved carried across the Atlantic.
