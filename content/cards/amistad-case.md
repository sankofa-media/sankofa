By 1839 Spain had banned bringing new captives into Cuba, yet slavers still landed them in Havana. That year a group of Mende people seized in Sierra Leone was shipped there, and two Spaniards, José Ruiz and Pedro Montes, bought 53 of them and put them aboard the schooner Amistad. A few days out, in early July, a captive named Sengbe Pieh, also known as Joseph Cinqué, freed himself and the others. They killed the captain and the cook and ordered the buyers to sail for Africa. By day the ship ran east; by night the Spaniards turned it north. After about two months the US brig Washington seized it off Long Island, and the Africans were jailed in Connecticut on a murder charge. Spain wanted them back, and so did President Martin Van Buren. Abolitionists paid for lawyers, the federal courts in Connecticut found the Africans had been kidnapped, and the government appealed. In March 1841 the Supreme Court, after a long plea by the former president John Quincy Adams, declared them free. Late in 1841 the 35 survivors, the rest having died at sea or in prison, sailed on the Gentleman and reached Freetown in January 1842. The ruling left American slavery untouched, but it called kidnapped Africans who rose against their captors free people, not property.
