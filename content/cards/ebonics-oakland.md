African American English is the English spoken by many African Americans. Linguists call its everyday form African American Vernacular English; the name Ebonics joins "ebony" and "phonics". It is a dialect with consistent rules of its own, and some of them mark distinctions that standard English does not. "Billy don't be telling lies" uses an unchanging be to say that something happens again and again, which "Billy don't tell lies" does not quite say.

On 18 December 1996 the school board of Oakland, California, unanimously adopted a resolution written by its African American Task Force. It recognised Ebonics as the primary language of many of the district's Black students, called it "genetically based" and not a dialect of English, and promised to teach the children in it as well as in English. A national argument followed.

Linguists backed Oakland's aim. On 3 January 1997 the Linguistic Society of America resolved that the variety is "systematic and rule-governed" and called the decision to use it in teaching standard English "linguistically and pedagogically sound". On 15 January the board unanimously revised its text: the words "genetically based" and the promise to teach in Ebonics were gone, while the goal of helping students master standard English stayed. Later that month Oakland's superintendent and linguists defended the approach before a Senate subcommittee.

The uproar passed; the linguists' verdict stands: African American English is a rule-governed system, not broken English.
