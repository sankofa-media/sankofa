Swahili, or Kiswahili, is a Bantu language of the East African coast. Its grammar belongs to the great Bantu family of Africa, while its vocabulary carries the traces of centuries of trade across the Indian Ocean, above all a great many words taken from Arabic. For most of its history it was written in Arabic script, and the oldest Swahili writing on paper that can be firmly dated comes from the early eighteenth century.

In 1711 Fatima bint Muhammad, the sultana of Kilwa, a port town on the Swahili coast, wrote a letter in Swahili in Arabic letters. Her letter and others from the same years were seized by the Portuguese and ended up in their archives in Goa, in India, where they survive.

Inland, Swahili travelled along the caravan routes that carried ivory and captives deep into Central Africa, and it became a lingua franca for people of different origins. Colonial rulers then used it to govern, the Germans in Tanganyika among them.

Today more than 200 million people speak it. In 2021 UNESCO proclaimed 7 July World Kiswahili Language Day. A language once written by a sultana in a letter now links much of East, Central and Southern Africa.
