Nicaragua's Caribbean coast, long called the Mosquito Coast, is a world apart from the Spanish-speaking Pacific side of the country. Its largest Black community lives in and around the port of Bluefields. They are Creoles: Afro-Caribbean Nicaraguans who speak an English-based creole as their first language.

Their ancestors were brought to the coast as slaves by British settlers, to cut timber and work plantations. In the eighteenth century people who escaped slavery, some of them from Jamaica, built free communities around Bluefields and Pearl Lagoon. After Britain abolished slavery in the 1830s, freed people from across the British Caribbean joined them. The coast was then under British protection, beyond the reach of Nicaragua's government, and Creole merchants rose to run much of its trade and politics. From the 1880s American banana and lumber companies turned Bluefields into a boomtown.

In 1894 Nicaragua took control of the coast, and mestizos from the Pacific side replaced Creoles in the top posts. The Creoles stayed. Bluefields is still the heart of Nicaragua's Afro-Caribbean life, and in the villages along the lagoons Creoles live alongside Miskitu and [[garifuna|Garifuna]] neighbours.

The town shows how a free Black society took shape on the edge of empires and kept its own language through every change of flag.
