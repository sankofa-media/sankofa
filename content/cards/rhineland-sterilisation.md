After the First World War, France occupied the German Rhineland until 1930, and a large part of its troops came from its colonies, many of them from Africa. German women had children with some of these soldiers. A nationalist campaign denounced the colonial troops as a disgrace, and the press gave the children a demeaning name. Most of the children were German citizens through their mothers. Already under the Weimar Republic, officials considered sterilising them.

In 1937, on a secret order from Hitler, the Gestapo took the task in hand. Commissions with anthropologists examined the teenagers, and doctors sterilised them, illegally even under the Nazis' own sterilisation law of 1933. Hans Hauck, born in Frankfurt in 1920 to a German mother and an Algerian soldier, was one of them. He had to sign a pledge not to marry or have sex. He later served in the German army and was a Soviet prisoner of war until 1949.

After the war most of the victims received no compensation at all. Hauck was among the very few who spoke about it in public, in interviews late in his life.

The operation targeted German children for one reason: who their fathers were.
