The Combahee River Raid was a Union army operation in South Carolina during the Civil War, guided by Harriet Tubman, that freed hundreds of enslaved people in a single night. Tubman had escaped slavery in Maryland and become a well-known conductor on the Underground Railroad, a story later overgrown with [[underground-railroad-myths|myths]]. In 1863 she was a spy and scout for the army in Union-held Beaufort, and from people who had fled the rice plantations on the Combahee River she learned where Confederates had mined the river.

On the night of 1 June three gunboats left Beaufort carrying Black soldiers of the 2nd South Carolina Volunteers, Colonel James Montgomery and Tubman, and she guided them past the mines. Soldiers set plantation houses and rice mills on fire while enslaved people ran to the boats, and the gunboats' cannon drove off a Confederate attack. By the time they turned back, more than 700 people were free, and many of the freed men enlisted in the regiment that had come for them.

Tubman sought pay for decades; in 1899 Congress raised her pension to $20 a month, without recognizing her as a scout or raider. In 1974 Black feminists in Boston named themselves the Combahee River Collective, and their 1977 statement introduced the phrase "identity politics".
