Colonial Boston was a Puritan town in which a church congregation could buy a man as a gift for its pastor. In 1706 the congregation of the minister Cotton Mather bought him an enslaved West African man, and Mather named him Onesimus. Asked about smallpox, Onesimus said he had "undergone an Operation, which had given him something of the Small-Pox, and would forever preserve him from it". The practice, he said, was common among his people, whom Mather wrote down as the "Guramantese". In 1716 Mather described it in a letter to the Royal Society in London.

In 1721 a devastating epidemic struck Boston. Most doctors scorned inoculation, but Zabdiel Boylston took up Mather's arguments: he inoculated his own young son and enslaved people of his household, then hundreds more. The town turned on the two men, and someone threw an explosive through the window of Mather's house. Yet the count spoke for itself: about one in forty of Boylston's patients died, against about one in seven of the townspeople who caught the disease naturally.

Of Onesimus's later life little is known except that he partly bought his freedom, paying Mather towards the purchase of another enslaved person to replace him. The method that saved Boston that year was African knowledge, and he was the one who carried it there.
