In the 1940s major American tennis was closed to Black players, who competed in their own American Tennis Association. Althea Gibson, born in 1927 to sharecroppers in Silver, South Carolina, grew up in Harlem, where at twelve she won the New York City women's paddle-tennis championship. Neighbours and members of Harlem's Cosmopolitan Tennis Club paid for her junior membership, and from 1947 she won ten ATA national titles in a row.

In 1950, after the former champion Alice Marble argued in print that Gibson should be allowed to meet the best players on the court, she became the first Black player at the US National Championships. She won the French Championships in 1956, and in July 1957 she became the first Black woman to win Wimbledon, taking the trophy from Queen Elizabeth II; New York welcomed her home with a parade. In 1958 she won Wimbledon and the US title again.

Then, at the top of the world, she quit amateur tennis to earn a living, only to find that the women's professional game paid little. She tried singing, and in 1964, at 37, became the first African American woman to join the LPGA golf tour. She died in 2003, at 76.
