In 1444 Lançarote de Freitas, a tax official close to Prince Henry of Portugal, led six caravels from Lagos, a port on the Algarve coast, to the Arguin coast of today's Mauritania. Raids there took 235 people.

Early on 8 August, before the heat, the captives were brought ashore and placed in a field outside the town gate to be divided into fifths. The chronicler Gomes Eanes de Zurara wrote of what happened next with open pity: some stood with their faces bathed in tears, others cried out to heaven or lamented in a dirge after the custom of their country. Then the men in charge of the division began to part fathers from sons, husbands from wives, brothers from brothers. Townspeople and villagers left their work to watch. Henry sat on horseback and took 46 people as his royal fifth.

Lagos became a hub of this early trade. Rescue excavations for an underground car park outside the medieval walls later uncovered more than 150 skeletons from the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, many with signs of African ancestry. Zurara's pages and those bones are among the earliest records of a trade that later carried millions across the Atlantic. Soon the centre of the Portuguese traffic moved to Lisbon, where Africans would build [[mocambo-lisbon|a neighbourhood of their own]].
