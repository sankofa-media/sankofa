Mapungubwe Hill rises near the point where the Shashe River joins the Limpopo, where South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe now meet. In the 13th century it was the capital of a kingdom that traded gold and ivory towards the Indian Ocean. Its rulers lived on top of the hill, walled off from the commoners below, and were buried there with gold.

In 1932 a local man led the prospector E. S. J. van Graan, his son and three others up the hill. They found gold beads and bangles, dug into a grave, and among the gold were small rhinoceroses. The party shared out the treasure, but van Graan's son, who had studied at the University of Pretoria, alerted the university, which tracked down the pieces and took over the digging.

The best known of them is a rhinoceros made of thin gold foil, shaped over a core of carved wood. The find was presented in the press as a mystery, and the university's excavations were kept fairly quiet until after the change of government in 1994.

Today the rhino is kept at the University of Pretoria. It stands at the centre of the badge of the Order of Mapungubwe, one of South Africa's national orders.
