Mapungubwe Hill rises near the point where the Shashe River joins the Limpopo, where South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe now meet. In the 13th century it was the capital of a kingdom that traded gold and ivory towards the Indian Ocean. Its rulers lived on top of the hill, walled off from the commoners below, and were buried there with gold.

In December 1932 a local man showed the prospector E. S. J. van Graan, his son and three others the hidden way up the hill. On top they found gold, dug into graves, and among the gold was a small rhinoceros. The party shared out the treasure, but van Graan's son, who had studied at the University of Pretoria, contacted his former professor, Leo Fouché. The university tracked down the pieces, and in June 1933 the government bought the farm and gave the university the right to excavate.

Accounts differ on whether the rhinoceros came out in that first dig or in the university's work of 1933. It is made of thin gold foil pressed over a carved wooden figure and held with tiny gold nails; the wood has long since decayed.

The rhino is kept at the University of Pretoria and stands at the centre of the badge of the Order of Mapungubwe, a South African national order.
