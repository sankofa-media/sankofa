In British Guiana, on the northern coast of South America, slavery ended in two steps. From 1834 the enslaved were held as "apprentices", and on 1 August 1838 they became fully free.

In November 1839, 83 freed men and women from five estates on the East Coast of Demerara, Dochfour, Ann's Grove, Hope, Paradise and Enmore, pooled their money and bought a whole plantation, Northbrook, for 30,000 guilders, about $10,000. Village tradition holds that they brought the money in a wheelbarrow. They renamed their land Victoria, in honour of the queen, and in 1845 they built the Wilberforce Congregational Church, named after the British abolitionist.

Victoria was the first village the freed Africans of the colony bought for themselves, and it set off what Guyanese call the Village Movement, as group after group of former slaves bought estates of their own.

It turned freedom on paper into land owned in common. Victoria still stands on the East Coast of Demerara, and so does its Emancipation Hut, a small wooden building that has kept its shape since the purchase.
