Arabic Afrikaans is Afrikaans written in the Arabic alphabet. In the nineteenth century the Muslims of the Cape used it to write their everyday language for religious teaching. Arabic has few vowel signs and lacks many Afrikaans sounds, so teachers and imams bent its letters and vowel marks to fit a language they were not made for.

The Cape's Muslim community grew largely from enslaved people and exiles brought from the islands of Southeast Asia. Tuan Guru, banished to the Cape in 1780, opened a madrasah at the end of the eighteenth century and taught in Malay. By the middle of the nineteenth century the pupils' home language, Cape Dutch, the early form of Afrikaans, had replaced Malay in the schools, and children copied their lessons into notebooks in Afrikaans, in Arabic script.

In 1862 the Ottoman scholar Abu Bakr Effendi arrived in Cape Town, learned Afrikaans and wrote Bayān al-Dīn, an explanation of the faith. Its pages first went round by hand; in 1877 it was printed in Constantinople. It is the earliest Arabic Afrikaans book that survives. From 1898 the community's religious books began to appear in Latin letters instead.

These notebooks and books show that some of the first written Afrikaans came from Muslims descended from the enslaved, which undoes the apartheid-era myth that Afrikaans was a white language only.
