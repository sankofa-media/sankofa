Arabic Afrikaans is Afrikaans written in the Arabic alphabet. In the nineteenth century the Muslims of the Cape used it to write their everyday language for religious teaching. Arabic had no signs for many Afrikaans sounds, above all its vowels, so scholars and teachers bent its letters and vowel marks to fit a language they were not made for.

The Cape's Muslim community grew largely from enslaved people and political exiles whom the Dutch brought from Southeast Asia. Tuan Guru, sent to the Cape as a state prisoner in 1780, opened a madrasah in Dorp Street at the end of the eighteenth century and taught in Malay. By the middle of the nineteenth century Cape Dutch, the early form of Afrikaans that the children already spoke, had replaced Malay in the schools, and pupils copied their lessons into notebooks in Afrikaans, in Arabic script.

In the 1860s the Ottoman scholar Abu Bakr Effendi came to Cape Town, learned Afrikaans and by 1869 had written Bayān al-Dīn, an exposition of the faith. Its pages first went round by hand; in 1877 it was printed in Constantinople. It is the earliest Arabic Afrikaans book that survives.

These notebooks and books show that some of the first written Afrikaans came from Muslims descended from the enslaved, which undoes the apartheid-era myth that Afrikaans was a white language only.
