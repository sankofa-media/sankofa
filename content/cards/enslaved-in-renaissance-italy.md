At the end of the fifteenth century Venice was one of the great trading cities of the Mediterranean, and the Rialto was its commercial heart. In the mid-1490s Vittore Carpaccio painted the Miracle of the Relic of the Cross at the Rialto Bridge, now in the Gallerie dell'Accademia. The canvas is crowded with daily life: Venetians in their finery, merchants from many lands, gondolas on the Grand Canal. Among the gondoliers are Black Africans.

Many Black Africans in Renaissance Venice had arrived enslaved, carried to Europe by a trade that grew after the Portuguese began [[lagos-1444|seizing people on the West African coast]] in the mid-fifteenth century. In the city many enslaved men served patrician households, often as boatmen. Yet slavery in Venice was not fixed to skin colour as it later was in the American South: many Black Venetians were free. The historian Kate Lowe, reading the records of the ferrymen's guilds, found Black Africans among their members, men who had moved from slavery into a trade of their own.

Whether Carpaccio's gondoliers were enslaved servants or freed professionals cannot be known. Scholars now read such paintings together with guild and court records, and the Black men at the oars have become a way into lives the archives rarely named.
