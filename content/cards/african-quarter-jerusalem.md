In the Old City of Jerusalem, right beside the Council Gate, one of the entrances to the al-Aqsa mosque compound, stand two massive medieval buildings that the Ottomans turned into prisons. They are home to a small community of Afro-Palestinians: a few hundred people, around fifty families.

Most of them descend from African Muslims from Chad, Sudan, Nigeria and Senegal who crossed the continent on pilgrimage to Mecca and went on to pray at al-Aqsa. Some arrived before 1948 and could not go home once that year's war closed the borders; others came as volunteers to fight in it. Many worked as guards at the mosque compound, and the Islamic trust that owns the old prisons let the Africans live in them. The men married Palestinian women, and their children grew up Palestinian.

Their grandchildren call themselves Afro-Palestinians. Some joined armed Palestinian groups after Israel took East Jerusalem in 1967 and spent long years in Israeli prisons. Today the community runs its own African Community Society, passes down the stories of how the grandfathers came to Jerusalem, and lives, as its families always have, a few steps from the gate.
