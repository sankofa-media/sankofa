Bilal ibn Rabah lived in Mecca in the early seventh century, a trading town where a merchant named Muhammad had begun to preach one God. Bilal's parents came from Abyssinia, today's Ethiopia, and he himself was enslaved. He was one of the very first people in Mecca to follow the new faith.

His master was Umayya ibn Khalaf. According to Ibn Ishaq, Muhammad's eighth-century biographer, Umayya would take Bilal out at the hottest hour of the day, lay him on his back in the open valley and have a great rock put on his chest until he denied Muhammad. Bilal would not. Abu Bakr, Muhammad's closest companion and later the first caliph, took him away from Umayya and freed him.

In Medina, after a follower dreamed that the faithful should be summoned by a human voice, Muhammad chose Bilal to give the call, the adhan, because his voice carried further. He became the first muezzin. When the Muslims took Mecca in 630, Muhammad had Bilal call the prayer at the Kaaba itself. In the end he went north with the Muslim armies to Syria and stayed there for the rest of his life.

Every muezzin who climbs a minaret today continues the office that began with an Abyssinian freedman's voice.
