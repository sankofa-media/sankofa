In April 1963 local activists in Birmingham, Alabama, and [[martin-luther-king|Martin Luther King]]'s Southern Christian Leadership Conference began a campaign of sit-ins, pickets and marches against the city's segregation laws. A court injunction banned the protests. On Good Friday, 12 April, King marched anyway and was arrested.

In his cell he was handed a newspaper that carried a statement by eight white clergymen calling the demonstrations "unwise and untimely". King began his answer in the newspaper's margins, went on writing on scraps of paper and finished on pads left by his lawyers, who carried the pages out of the jail. Dated 16 April, the letter explained why Black Americans could not keep waiting for their rights: "This 'Wait' has almost always meant 'Never.'"

After eight days King was released. Within weeks the letter was circulating as a pamphlet and in newspapers and magazines across the country, and in 1964 it went into his book on the campaign, Why We Can't Wait.

Written fast, in a cell, to a handful of ministers, it is now counted as the most important written document of the civil rights movement, anthologised and studied for generations.
