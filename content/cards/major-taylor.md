In the 1890s bicycle racing was one of the most popular sports in America and Europe. Marshall Taylor was born in Indianapolis in 1878. As a boy he did bicycle stunts outside a bike shop dressed in a military uniform, and the name "Major" stuck. The racer and manufacturer Louis "Birdie" Munger took him on, and Taylor followed Munger to Worcester, Massachusetts, where he found a friendlier welcome.

Races in the South barred him, and white riders did not want him in the field. In 1899 in Montreal he won the world one-mile sprint championship, only the second Black world champion in any sport, after the boxer George Dixon. A devout Baptist, he would not race on Sundays, and he went to Europe only in 1901. There he beat every European champion who took him on.

He retired in 1910. He lost his fortune, and ended up in Chicago selling his self-published autobiography. He died in the charity ward of Cook County Hospital in 1932, at 53, and was buried in an unmarked grave. In 1948 former racers, with money from the bicycle maker Frank Schwinn, moved him to a marked one.

In the most popular sport of its day, the fastest man on two wheels was a Black man who had to race the colour line as well.
