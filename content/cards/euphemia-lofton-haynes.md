Euphemia Lofton Haynes was born in Washington, D.C., in 1890. She graduated from Smith College in 1914 and then taught in the city's public schools for 47 years, from first-grade classes to Dunbar High School, where she chaired the mathematics department. In 1930 she founded the mathematics department at Miner Teachers College, today the University of the District of Columbia.

All the while she kept studying. In 1943 the Catholic University of America awarded her a doctorate for a thesis whose title runs like a sentence: The Determination of Sets of Independent Conditions Characterizing Certain Special Cases of Symmetric Correspondences. She was the first African American woman to earn a PhD in mathematics.

Haynes did not stop at the blackboard. She sat on the District's Board of Education from 1960 to 1968, and from 1966 to 1967 was its president, the first woman to lead it. She attacked the "track system", which sorted pupils into academic or vocational streams and was said to lock Black and poor children out of real schooling, and she backed Julius Hobson's lawsuit against it. In June 1967 a federal judge abolished the tracks. She died in 1980 and left $700,000 to Catholic University for a professorship and a student loan fund.
