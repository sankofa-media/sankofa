In the eighteenth century Britain's colonies ran on slavery, but English law had never settled whether a person could be held as a slave in England itself. James Somerset was born in Africa and in 1749 was sold in Virginia to the merchant Charles Steuart, or Stewart. In 1769 Steuart brought him to London. There Somerset was baptised, and on 1 October 1771 he left his master. Steuart had him seized and held aboard the ship Ann and Mary on the Thames, to be taken out of England and sold. Somerset's three godparents applied to the Court of King's Bench for a writ of habeas corpus, and the campaigner [[granville-sharp|Granville Sharp]] took up his cause. On 22 June 1772 the Chief Justice, Lord Mansfield, ruled that Somerset could not be forcibly removed, and he walked free. The judgment was narrowly worded, yet it was widely taken to mean that slavery could not be enforced in England. Days later a London paper reported that nearly two hundred Black Londoners had celebrated at a Westminster public house, drinking Lord Mansfield's health and ending the evening with a ball. By September the story had been reprinted in Massachusetts newspapers, carrying the news of Somerset's victory across the Atlantic.
