In the rainforest of south-western Nigeria, around the Yoruba town of Ijebu-Ode, a great trench with an earthen bank beside it runs for some 160 kilometres, enclosing the heartland of the old Ijebu kingdom. In places the trench is 15 metres deep. Archaeologists describe it as the largest single monument in Africa and the largest known single earthwork in the world.

Its age is disputed: an early radiocarbon study pointed to the centuries around 800–1000, while a current archaeological project dates it to the late fourteenth century. Either way, digging it by hand took organised labour on a vast scale, the work of a state able to command many people.

Ijebu tradition gives it a founder. It remembers Bilikisu Sungbo, a wealthy, childless noblewoman, and says the rampart was built as her memorial; some also identify her with the biblical Queen of Sheba, a link that rests on legend alone. Her grave is honoured in a sacred grove at Oke-Eri, beside the earthwork.

Much of the Eredo now lies under dense forest, yet it is still a living landscape: villages along it keep shrines linked to the earthwork, and archaeologists are only beginning to map its full course.
