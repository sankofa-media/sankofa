In 1860, more than fifty years after the United States outlawed the international slave trade, the Mobile shipbuilder Timothy Meaher wagered he could smuggle in a cargo of captives. Captain William Foster took about 110 men, women and children aboard at Ouidah in Dahomey, a hub of [[aro-and-dahomey|the African side of the trade]], then burned the schooner Clotilda to hide the evidence. Only Foster was fined. The captives stayed enslaved until 1865.

{male} Oluale Kossola was nineteen when Dahomey's army raided his Yoruba town of Bantè. Once free, he married his shipmate Abile. When the Africans could not raise the fare home, Kossola put their claim to Meaher: you took us from our land and home, so give us land for an African Town. Meaher refused. In 1927 [[hurston|Zora Neale Hurston]] came to interview him and kept returning; he told her about his town, the raid and the slave pen at Ouidah. By then he had outlived his wife and all six of their children. He died in 1935, and her book Barracoon waited until 2018.

{female} One captive, Redoshi, was twelve. Traders made her the "wife" of a grown man from another people and sold the pair to Washington Smith of Dallas County, who renamed her Sally Smith. Freed after five years in house and fields, she sharecropped his land with her husband, Yawith, and kept her language and faith. She told her story to Amelia Boynton Robinson, later a Selma voting-rights leader, and outlived all but one known shipmate. A 1938 film shows her lips moving without sound, the only known footage of a woman survivor of the transatlantic slave trade. The scholar Hannah Durkin reconstructed her life in 2019.

The shipmates near Mobile bought land, partly from Meaher, and built their own African Town; many descendants still live in today's Africatown. In May 2019 the Alabama Historical Commission identified the wreck, and the ship meant to hide the crime became its proof.
