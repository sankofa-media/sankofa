The Clotilda was a schooner that smuggled African captives into Alabama in 1860, half a century after the United States had banned the slave trade. The Mobile shipbuilder Timothy Meaher had wagered that he could land a cargo of captives in Mobile Bay. Captain William Foster sailed to Ouidah in Dahomey, a hub of [[aro-and-dahomey|the African side of the trade]], and bought, by most counts, 110 men, women and children. Back in Alabama, Foster burned the ship to hide the evidence. The authorities learned of the voyage, but Meaher was never punished; only Foster paid a fine. The captives stayed enslaved until 1865. Once free, they saved to go home to Africa but could not raise enough. Oluale Kossola, called Cudjo Lewis, asked Meaher for a piece of land on which to build an African Town; Meaher refused, so the survivors bought land from him and others and built it anyway. In 1927 [[hurston|Zora Neale Hurston]] interviewed Kossola. He died in 1935, and her book Barracoon waited until 2018. In May 2019 the Alabama Historical Commission identified the wreck. Many descendants still live in the town, now called Africatown, and the ship meant to hide the crime became its proof, a late episode in a trade that [[slavevoyages|SlaveVoyages]] counts voyage by voyage.
