Before electronic computers could be trusted, American flight research ran on human "computers". Katherine Johnson, born in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia, in 1918, was a maths teacher when she heard that the NACA's Langley laboratory in Virginia was hiring for West Area Computing, its all-Black section of human computers. She started there in 1953, and when the NACA became NASA she worked out trajectories, including for Alan Shepard's flight in May 1961, the first American in space.

In February 1962 John Glenn was to orbit the Earth in Friendship 7, on a trajectory computed by electronic computers. Before climbing aboard, Glenn asked that Johnson personally check the machines' numbers by hand. She did, and Glenn flew.

She went on to work on Apollo, retired in 1986 after 33 years, and in 2015, at 97, received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. The book and film Hidden Figures made her name known far beyond NASA. She died in 2020, at 101.
