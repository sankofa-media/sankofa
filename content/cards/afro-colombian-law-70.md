Colombia's Pacific lowlands, the rainforest of the Chocó and the coast south towards Ecuador, are home to rural Black communities who live along the rivers. Slavery in Colombia ended in 1851, but for well over a century afterwards the land these communities lived on counted in law as vacant state land, and they owned none of it.

The change came out of the 1991 Constitution. After years of pressure from Black and peasant organisations, it included a transitional article 55 that promised them rights to their territories. On 27 August 1993 Congress turned that promise into Law 70. It recognised the right of Black communities living on vacant riverside land in the Pacific basin, according to their traditional ways of farming, to own it collectively. Each community was to form a Consejo Comunitario, a community council, to receive the title and govern the land, and the law also set out to protect their cultural identity and their development as an ethnic group.

Title by title, the councils became owners. Today they hold nearly six million hectares, most of it on the Pacific coast.

Before 1991 the Colombian state barely acknowledged that Afro-Colombian peoples existed. Law 70 recognised them as an ethnic group with a right to the land they had lived on for generations.
