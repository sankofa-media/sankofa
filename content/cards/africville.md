Africville was a Black village on the southern shore of Bedford Basin, at the northern edge of Halifax, Nova Scotia. Among the first families on that shore were Black refugees of the War of 1812, many of them formerly enslaved. In 1848 two Black settlers, William Brown and William Arnold, bought land on the basin, and the Seaview African United Baptist Church became the heart of the village that grew there.

Residents paid city taxes, but Halifax never gave them running water or sewers. Instead it placed an infectious diseases hospital, a prison and, in the 1950s, the city dump beside them. In 1964 the city began moving the residents out. Houses were bulldozed as soon as families left, some people were carried to their new homes with their belongings in city garbage trucks, and the church was knocked down in the middle of the night in 1967. The last house fell in January 1970; about 400 people had lost their village.

Former residents and their children never stopped demanding redress. On 24 February 2010 Halifax mayor Peter Kelly apologised, and a replica of Seaview church, now the Africville Museum, opened on the site in 2012.
