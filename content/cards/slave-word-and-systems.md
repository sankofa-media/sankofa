The English word "slave", Middle English sclave, came through Old French esclave from Medieval Latin sclavus, which first meant "Slav". So many Slavs were enslaved in early medieval central Europe that their name came to mean an owned person. Europeans were still captured later: from the fifteenth to the eighteenth century raiders from the Black Sea steppe seized people in the lands of Poland, Lithuania, Ukraine and Russia and sold them through Caffa in Crimea to markets across the Ottoman Empire.

The Atlantic system differed in three ways. It was tied to descent: in Renaissance Venice a Black person could be enslaved or free, but in the Americas slavery came to be fixed to African ancestry and colour. It was hereditary by law: in 1662 Virginia declared that every child born in the colony would be "bond or free only according to the condition of the mother", reversing the English rule that a child followed its father. And it was industrial in scale: between 1500 and 1866 traders forced about 12.5 million Africans aboard ships bound for the Americas, and about 10.7 million survived the crossing.

What the Atlantic made of the word was new: slavery passed on [[partus-1662|through the mother]] and marked by colour, recorded voyage by voyage in [[slavevoyages|the traders' own ledgers]].
