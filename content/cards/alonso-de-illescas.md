In the sixteenth century the coast of Esmeraldas, in today's north-western Ecuador, was forest and river country whose Indigenous peoples had never submitted to Spain. In 1553 a ship from Panama ran aground there, and a group of enslaved Africans, seventeen men and six women by later accounts, escaped into the forest. One of them, Alonso de Illescas, had been born in Cape Verde and taken to Seville as a boy, where he received his master's name and learned Spanish, the Catholic faith and Spanish law. On the coast he used marriage as diplomacy: he wed the daughter of an Indigenous chief, and ties of kinship made him leader of Africans and Indigenous people alike. Their mixed descendants were called zambos, and the province is remembered as the Kingdom of the Zambos. Spanish military expeditions failed, so the Audiencia of Quito negotiated instead: a royal provision pardoned him and his family and named him governor, in effect recognising a community founded by people who had freed themselves. Illescas did not live to see the final settlement; around 1600 his son Sebastián went to Quito and made peace with the Crown. In 1997 Ecuador's Congress declared him a national hero and created a national day of the Afro-Ecuadorian people.
