Gnawa is the music and healing ritual of a Sufi brotherhood in Morocco. The Gnawa descend from sub-Saharan Africans brought to Morocco, mostly as slaves, by the 16th century at the latest, and their songs invoke ancestors, saints and spirits.

The sound rests on two instruments. The master, the maalem, plays the guembri, a three-string bass lute; around him the singers clatter qraqeb, heavy metal castanets. Its true home is the lila, an all-night ceremony in which the music calls the spirits, dancers fall into trance and the possessed are healed.

For a long time the Gnawa were a marginalised group in Morocco, but their music drew listeners from far away. The Brooklyn jazz pianist Randy Weston, who travelled through Africa on State Department tours, settled in Morocco at the end of the 1960s, drawn by the spiritual depth he heard in Gnawa. Today Gnawa groups gather every June in Essaouira for a festival on the harbour square, and women have taken up the guembri too: Asmaa Hamzaoui leads an all-female band called Bnat Timbouktou, the Daughters of Timbuktu.

In 2019 UNESCO inscribed Gnawa on its list of humanity's intangible heritage: a music made by people once enslaved is now counted as part of Morocco's own identity.
