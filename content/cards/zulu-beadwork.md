In the region now called KwaZulu-Natal, in South Africa, Zulu girls and women made beadwork that spoke. Beading was women's work. In the late 19th century, as tiny European glass beads became plentiful through trade, beadwork took on a role in courtship. Its colours and shapes could tell whether a woman was single, promised or married, and where she came from.

Courtship ran through beads. A young woman could give beadwork to a man she favoured, and by wearing it he showed that he returned her feelings. As a relationship grew, the pieces grew more elaborate, among them the small panels Europeans call "love letters". Each colour had two faces: red could stand for passion or for anger, yellow for fertility or for thirst and withering. Only white was always good. The order and dominance of colours could change the message entirely.

There was never a fixed dictionary. Museum curators stress that these "letters" cannot be read with one key: meanings varied from region to region and changed over time.

Read in its own place and time, Zulu beadwork is less a cipher than a local language of feeling, spoken by the women who made it.
