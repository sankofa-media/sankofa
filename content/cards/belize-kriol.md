Kriol is the creole language of Belize, on the Caribbean coast of Central America. Most of its words come from English, but its grammar follows African patterns. It is the language of Belize's Creole people and also the one Belizeans of other backgrounds use with one another. Its nearest relatives are the English creoles of the western Caribbean, such as [[jamaican-patois|Jamaican Patois]].

The language grew out of the timber trade. From the seventeenth century British woodcutters, the Baymen, cut timber along the Belize River, first logwood and later mahogany, and by the 1720s they were bringing enslaved Africans from Jamaica to do the cutting in camps in the forest. From the mixing of Africans and British settlers came the Creoles, and their speech became Kriol.

From the 1990s its speakers set out to write it down. Working with the linguist Ken Decker, the Belize Kriol Project developed a way to spell it, and in the 2000s Kriol activists published a grammar, The Song of Kriol, and a Kriol–English dictionary.

A language made by people who had no say over their own lives now has its own grammar, dictionary and spelling, as Belize's own.
