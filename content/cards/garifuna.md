Garifuna is the language of the Garifuna people, who live in towns and villages along the Caribbean coast of Honduras, Guatemala, Nicaragua and Belize, Dangriga among them, and in communities in the United States. The Garifuna are partly of African descent, but their language comes from the indigenous peoples of the Caribbean: it belongs to the Arawakan family, with a heavy overlay of Carib. It lives in a rich tradition of songs, tales and dances.

The people took shape on the island of Saint Vincent, where indigenous Caribbean and African ancestors became one population. In 1797 the British exiled them from Saint Vincent and shipped them to the coast of Honduras. From there they spread along the Caribbean shore of Central America, taking the language with them.

In 2001, when UNESCO named its first nineteen masterpieces of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity, the language, dance and music of the Garifuna were on the list. UNESCO warned at the same time that migration, discrimination and the language's near absence from schools threatened its survival. A people uprooted by an empire had carried its words across the sea, and the world finally counted them as a treasure.
