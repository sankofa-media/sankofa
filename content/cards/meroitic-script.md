Meroitic is the writing of the kingdom of Kush, whose capital Meroë stood on the Nile in what is now Sudan, some 200 km from Khartoum. It appeared around 200 BCE and stayed in use until after the fall of Meroë in the 4th century CE. It had two forms: hieroglyphs modelled on Egyptian ones, kept for royal and religious monuments, and a cursive derived from Egyptian demotic, used for almost everything else. Each has 23 signs, usually written right to left, with dots between words and phrases. It is an alphasyllabary: a consonant sign carries an inherent a unless a vowel sign follows it.

The script was cracked around 1910 by the British Egyptologist Francis Llewellyn Griffith, working from the many funerary inscriptions. Since then Meroitic can be read aloud. Understanding it is another matter: no bilingual text has been found, and beyond names, titles and a handful of words it remains largely untranslated.

The search for its relatives has narrowed: some linguists now place Meroitic in the Eastern Sudanic branch of Nilo-Saharan, close to Nubian. After Meroë fell, Christian Nubians wrote their own language, [[old-nubian|Old Nubian]], in an alphabet that borrowed a few letters from Meroitic.

Its signs can be read aloud today; what they say is still waiting to be understood.
