By the late 1930s William Grant Still, born in Woodville, Mississippi, in 1895 and raised in Little Rock, Arkansas, had already made history in American classical music. His Afro-American Symphony was the first symphony by an African American played by a major orchestra, and in 1936, leading the Los Angeles Philharmonic, he became the first African American to conduct a major American orchestra.

He then wrote an opera set in Haiti, about Jean-Jacques Dessalines, a leader of the [[haitian-revolution|Haitian Revolution]]. The libretto was by the poet Langston Hughes, with Still's wife, Verna Arvey. The score was finished by the end of the 1930s; then Still spent about ten years looking for a company that would stage it.

New York City Opera opened Troubled Island at City Center on 31 March 1949. It was the first opera by an African American composer produced by a major company in the United States. The first-night audience called the cast back for curtain call after curtain call, but most critics dismissed the opera, and the production closed after three performances.

Still went on composing until his death in Los Angeles in 1978. The opera he had fought a decade to stage has almost never been heard since: a first that opened a door and then saw it close.
