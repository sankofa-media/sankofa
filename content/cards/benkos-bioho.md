At the turn of the seventeenth century Cartagena de Indias was the chief port through which enslaved Africans entered Spanish South America. Benkos Biohó was brought there from the Bijagós Islands, in today's Guinea-Bissau, and baptised Domingo. In 1599 he escaped from his owner, Juan Gómez, with his wife, a daughter and about thirty others. They fortified a camp in the swamps of La Matuna, from which they raided estates and cattle herds. The colonial authorities could not destroy the palenque, so they negotiated. Its people were recognised as free and allowed to enter Cartagena; in return they promised not to take in new runaways. Biohó, whom people called a king, of the thick forest or of La Matuna, now walked the city dressed as a Spanish gentleman, with cloak and sword. The peace did not hold. In 1619 a clash with the guards at the port led to his arrest, and on 16 March 1621 he was hanged in Cartagena. His name outlived him: for two centuries rebels across the region took it up, and it became a byword for African resistance. [[san-basilio-de-palenque|San Basilio de Palenque]], the one maroon village that survives near Cartagena, honours him as its founder.
