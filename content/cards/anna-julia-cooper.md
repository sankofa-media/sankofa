Anna Julia Haywood was born enslaved in Raleigh, North Carolina, in 1858; her mother was enslaved, and her father was probably the man who held them. Emancipation brought new schools for freed people, and as a child she entered one of them, Saint Augustine's in Raleigh. There she noticed that boys were steered into harder courses than girls; she spent the rest of her life fighting for the education of Black women.

Widowed two years after an 1877 marriage, she took a mathematics degree at Oberlin College in 1884, later a master's, and taught at the M Street High School in Washington. In 1892 she published A Voice from the South, a founding text of Black feminist thought, and in 1900 she addressed the first Pan-African Conference in London. As principal of M Street she sent graduates to Ivy League colleges; her insistence on college preparation rather than vocational training cost her the post.

She raised five children of her late brother's family. In 1925, in her sixties, she received a doctorate from the University of Paris for a thesis written in French on slavery and the French Revolution, becoming the fourth African American woman to earn a PhD. She later led Frelinghuysen University, a Washington school for working Black adults, and died in 1964, aged 105.
