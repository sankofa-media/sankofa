In Georgia before the Civil War the law set out to keep Black people from schooling. Susie Baker was born into slavery there in 1848 and grew up with her grandmother in Savannah, where she learned to read in two secret schools kept by Black women. In April 1862, as Union guns took Fort Pulaski outside the city, her uncle led the family to the Union fleet; she was fourteen. On St. Simons Island officers discovered that she could read and asked her to open a school, and she taught about forty children by day and adults at night. There she married Edward King, a Black sergeant, and for the rest of the war she moved with his regiment, the 1st South Carolina Volunteers, later the 33rd US Colored Troops. She was its laundress and nurse, and she taught soldiers to read and write when they were off duty. After the war she opened a school for freed children in Savannah, but Edward died in 1866 and in time she had to take work as a servant. In the 1870s she settled in Boston, where she married Russell Taylor. In 1902 she published Reminiscences of My Life in Camp, the only memoir of the war by an African American woman. She died in 1912.
