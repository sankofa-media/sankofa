The Samaná Americans are a community on the Samaná peninsula in the north-east of the Dominican Republic. Their story begins in the 1820s, when the whole island of Hispaniola was ruled by Haiti, the Black republic born of revolution.

In 1824–25 free Black Americans from the mid-Atlantic coast of the United States left the country of their birth and emigrated to Haiti. They brought their faith with them, above all the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and their language.

The settlement on the Samaná peninsula held. Its families stayed together around their Protestant churches, and English remained the language of home for generations. The descendants still carry North American surnames such as Willmore, and the AME church is still part of their life.

Today they are Dominicans with a double memory: the heirs of free Black Americans who went looking for a home in the Black republic, whose churches and speech go back two centuries to the young United States.
