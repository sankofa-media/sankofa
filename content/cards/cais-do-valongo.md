Over three centuries Brazil took in more enslaved Africans than any other country in the Americas, some four million people. In 1811 Rio de Janeiro built a stone wharf at Valongo, on the city's harbour, for landing them. Around it stood warehouses, markets, a quarantine station and a cemetery. In the years that followed, somewhere between half a million and a million captives came ashore on its stones.

In 1843 the wharf was paved over with a grander one to welcome Teresa Cristina, the bride of Emperor Pedro II, and renamed the Empress Wharf. Later both vanished under the earth of the growing city.

In 2011 building works for the 2016 Olympics opened the ground. Archaeologists led by Tania Andrade Lima found the original paving of irregular stones, laid straight on the beach sand, and thousands of objects that had belonged to the captives or been taken from them: amulets, rings, shells and objects of African worship. In 2017 UNESCO made Valongo a World Heritage Site as the most important physical trace of the arrival of enslaved Africans in the Americas. Its remains now lie open in a city square, an open-air memorial to the people who stepped ashore there.
