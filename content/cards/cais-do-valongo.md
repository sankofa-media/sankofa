Over three centuries Brazil took in more enslaved Africans than any other country in the Americas, some four million people. In 1811 Rio de Janeiro built a stone wharf at Valongo, on the city's harbour, for landing them. Around it stood warehouses, markets, a quarantine station and a cemetery. Over the next two decades somewhere between half a million and a million captives came ashore on its stones.

In 1843 the wharf was paved over with a grander one to welcome Teresa Cristina, the bride of Emperor Pedro II, and renamed the Empress Wharf. Later landfill buried both.

In 2011 building works for the 2016 Olympics opened the ground. Archaeologists led by Tania Andrade Lima found the original paving of irregular stones, laid straight on the beach sand, and tens of thousands of objects that had belonged to the captives or been taken from them: charms, rings, stones used in African worship and cowrie shells, then a currency in Africa. In 2017 UNESCO made Valongo a World Heritage Site as the most important physical trace of the arrival of enslaved Africans in the Americas. Its remains now lie open in a city square, an open-air memorial to the people who stepped ashore there.
