In the 1840s the sugar estates of Matanzas, in western Cuba, ran on the labour of enslaved Africans. Among them were people from Yoruba lands, whom Cubans called Lucumí; their language survives as [[lucumi|Lucumí]]. Carlota was a Lucumí woman enslaved at the Triunvirato mill.

On 5 November 1843 she was among the leaders of a rising there. Beside her was Fermina, from the neighbouring Acana estate, who had been chained up after an earlier revolt and then freed; accounts differ on how. The rebels set fire to buildings, killed overseers and moved from estate to estate, freeing others as they went.

The governor's troops hunted them down. Carlota was killed and her body was torn apart. Fermina was shot by a firing squad in March 1844. That year the colony unleashed a wave of brutal repression against enslaved and free people of colour.

Revolutionary Cuba later made Carlota a national symbol of rebellion against oppression. When it sent troops to Angola in the mid-1970s, the military mission was named Operation Carlota in her honour, tying a revolt of enslaved Africans in Matanzas to Cuba's war in Africa.
