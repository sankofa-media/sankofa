In the Yoruba kingdom of Oyo, in what is now western Nigeria, custom required that when the king died, his horseman, the Elesin, would end his own life and follow his master into the world of the dead. In the 1940s, under British colonial rule, a district officer stepped in to stop one horseman from doing so. Wole Soyinka turned the episode into Death and the King's Horseman, written in 1975 while he was a fellow at Churchill College, Cambridge.

In the play the horseman, Elesin Oba, delays his passage to marry a young bride, and the British district officer Simon Pilkings, who sees the custom as barbaric, stops him from dying. Pilkings and his wife, meanwhile, dress for a fancy-dress ball in Yoruba masks of the dead. Then the Elesin's eldest son, a medical student in London, comes home, and the night ends in tragedy.

The play was first staged in Nigeria in 1976, and productions followed in the United States and Britain. It is often read as a "clash of cultures" between Yoruba tradition and colonial rule. Soyinka, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1986, has firmly rejected that reading: his play, he insists, is not about two civilisations colliding.
