San rock art is the painting and engraving of southern Africa's hunter-gatherers on the walls of rock shelters, made over thousands of years. Direct radiocarbon dating of carbon-black paint puts the oldest dated paintings, in south-eastern Botswana, at between about 5,700 and 4,400 years old. The Tsodilo Hills in the Kalahari, a World Heritage Site since 2001, hold more than 4,500 rock paintings. In the uKhahlamba-Drakensberg mountains, the eland stands at the centre of the art.

For a long time such images were taken as art for art's sake. In the 1970s and 1980s David Lewis-Williams, reading San testimony recorded in the 19th and 20th centuries, argued that they grew out of the trance dance. In San belief the eland held a powerful potency. Healers called on it to enter trance, and in trance they healed the sick and made rain. The eland was invoked too when a girl came of age.

His reading changed how the paintings are studied. A painted eland on a Drakensberg wall was far more than an animal to be hunted, and the painted shelters are now read as a record of the spiritual life of the San.
