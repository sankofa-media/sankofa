In 1876 the United States marked a hundred years of independence with the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia. Among the works sent over from Rome was a marble Cleopatra by Edmonia Lewis, the daughter of a Black father and an Ojibwe mother. At Oberlin College in Ohio, Lewis had been accused of poisoning two white classmates and beaten by a white mob; in 1865 she moved to Rome and began to carve marble.

Her Cleopatra is not weighing her fate. The queen sits on her throne just after death, still crowned, the asp in her hand. In Philadelphia the statue drew crowds and caused a stir.

Then the Chicago gambler "Blind John" Condon set it over the grave of his racehorse, also named Cleopatra, at his track in Forest Park, Illinois. It outlasted the track, a wartime Navy plant and a post office, and a fire inspector from nearby Cicero took it into his care. In 1988 the scholar Marilyn Richardson found it in a mall storeroom, crudely painted, among Christmas decorations.

In 1994 the Forest Park historical society gave it to the Smithsonian American Art Museum in Washington, where it is on view today. It is the most famous work of the first sculptor of African American and Native American descent to win international fame.
