In central Nigeria, people of what archaeologists call the Nok culture made fired-clay figures of people and animals during the first millennium BCE. They are the earliest large figurative sculptures known in Africa outside Egypt. Most show people with elaborate hairstyles, and their eyes are pierced right through.

The first piece, a small monkey head, turned up in 1928 in the tin mines around the village of Nok. In 1943 another head was found, one that had been serving as a scarecrow, and it reached the British archaeologist Bernard Fagg in Jos. Fagg went looking for more, gathered nearly 200 pieces in a few years and named the culture after Nok. At Taruga he found iron-smelting furnaces with figurines inside and around them.

His finds also made Nok terracottas prized by collectors in Europe and the United States, and illegal digging destroyed many sites. In the 2000s a Frankfurt team under Peter Breunig and Nicole Rupp, working with Nigerian researchers and local villagers, some of them former diggers, returned to excavate. Their radiocarbon dates push the figures back to about 900 BCE, and Nok holds some of the earliest evidence for iron working in West Africa.

The scarecrow head is now known as the Jemaa Head, after the village where it was found.
