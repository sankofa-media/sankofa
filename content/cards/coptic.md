Coptic is the final stage of the language of ancient Egypt, spoken from about the second century CE. Earlier Egyptian had been written in hieroglyphs and in the cursive hieratic and Demotic scripts. But written Demotic no longer matched the spoken language, and Egypt's Christians, for whom Greek was the language of their faith, wrote Egyptian in the Greek alphabet instead. For sounds that Greek lacked they added seven letters taken from Demotic. They also borrowed many Greek words for religious ideas.

Coptic had several dialects, the best known being Sahidic and Bohairic. As an everyday language it died out around the fourteenth century, but it lives on in the liturgy of the Coptic Church.

Centuries later it opened the past. Jean-François Champollion studied Coptic from his youth. When he worked on the Rosetta Stone, which carries the same text in Greek, Demotic and hieroglyphs, his Coptic let him hear the Egyptian words behind the signs: he saw that hieroglyphs also stood for sounds. In 1822 he succeeded in reading them.

So the last spoken form of Egyptian became the bridge back to its first writing, and the pharaohs' language is still heard at Coptic services.
