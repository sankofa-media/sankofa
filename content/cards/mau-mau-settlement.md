In October 1952 Britain's colonial government in Kenya declared a state of emergency against the Mau Mau rebellion. Political organisations were banned, and thousands of people were held in detention camps without trial and put through harsh so-called rehabilitation meant to break their nationalist loyalties.

Half a century later elderly survivors of the camps sued the British government at the High Court in London. Britain argued that any liability had passed to Kenya at independence in 1963, but in 2011 the court let the claims go ahead.

On 6 June 2013 the Foreign Secretary, William Hague, told Parliament that Kenyans had been subjected to torture and ill-treatment at the hands of the colonial administration, and that the British government "sincerely regrets" it. Britain settled the claims of 5,228 people for £19.9 million while still denying legal liability, and promised a memorial. On 12 September 2015 that British-funded memorial was unveiled in Nairobi: a statue of a Mau Mau fighter receiving supplies from a woman.
