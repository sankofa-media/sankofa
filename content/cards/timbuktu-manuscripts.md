Timbuktu, in today's Mali, grew in the Middle Ages as a trading town on the Niger's northern bend and became a centre of Islamic learning. Its great Djinguereber and Sankore mosques date from the 14th century, the age of Mali's ruler [[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], and became seats of scholarship. Its scholars wrote on law, mathematics and astronomy, and Timbuktu's families kept such books for centuries, in household libraries and later in the state Ahmed Baba Institute.

When armed jihadist groups took Timbuktu on 1 April 2012, its librarians smuggled the books out to Bamako, some by cart, some by boat on the Niger. The escape was organised by Abdel Kader Haidara, keeper of the Mamma Haidara library and head of SAVAMA-DCI. Hundreds of thousands of manuscripts were saved.

Before French and Malian troops retook the town in late January 2013, the militants set fire to the Ahmed Baba Institute, and some 4,000 of its manuscripts were burned or stolen. The rescued books stayed in Bamako, where conservators set to work on them.
