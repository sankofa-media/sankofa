"Mbube", Zulu for "lion", is a song that Solomon Linda, a Zulu migrant worker and singer, recorded in Johannesburg in 1939 with his choir, the Evening Birds. At the microphone Linda improvised a soaring falsetto line. Gallo Records released it, and it became a hit across South Africa. Linda signed his rights over to the company.

In the early 1950s the record reached Pete Seeger, who misheard the Zulu words and called his version "Wimoweh"; the Weavers took it onto the American charts. In 1961 new English lyrics turned it into "The Lion Sleeps Tonight", a number one for the Tokens; later it went into Disney's The Lion King. More than 150 artists have recorded the melody.

Linda received almost nothing, and he died in poverty in 1962. After a Rolling Stone exposé by Rian Malan described his daughters' poverty, the family sued in South Africa, invoking an imperial copyright rule that returned rights to an author's estate 25 years after death, and attached Disney's South African trademarks. In 2006 they settled with Abilene Music, the American publisher: Linda was credited as a co-composer, and his heirs received past and future royalties.

One of pop's best-known melodies began as a Zulu migrant's improvisation, and it took almost seventy years for his name to return to it.
