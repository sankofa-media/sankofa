The All-Negro Hour was a weekly radio variety show that first went out from station WSBC in Chicago on 3 November 1929. Radio then carried Black singers, but no Black voices speaking: no Black announcers, actors or hosts.

Its creator, Jack L. Cooper, born in 1888, had been a boxer and a vaudeville song-and-dance man, and wrote on the theatre for the [[chicago-defender|Chicago Defender]]. Sent by the paper to Washington in the mid-1920s, he got his first taste of radio there, on station WCAP. Back in Chicago he looked for a station that would carry a show by Black people for Black listeners. Joseph Silverstein, whose small WSBC sold airtime to the city's immigrant communities, agreed. The show offered music, comedy and serial sketches.

In 1932 a performer walked out over pay just before air time. Cooper set a microphone in front of a phonograph and played records, and kept on doing it. He is remembered as America's first Black disc jockey.

He built an empire from that hour. By the late 1940s his company produced more than 40 hours of programmes a week on four Chicago stations. He died in Chicago in 1970. His model of programming for Black listeners led to stations such as WDIA in Memphis, which in 1949 put an all-Black staff on the air.
