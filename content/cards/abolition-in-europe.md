Europe's empires did not end slavery at a stroke. They did it one by one over almost a century, usually in two steps, first the trade and then slavery itself, and usually with the owners' losses in mind.

Denmark was the first country to ban the transatlantic slave trade, in 1792, though the ban took effect only in 1803. Revolutionary France abolished slavery in 1794; Napoleon restored it in 1802, and it ended for good only in 1848. Britain's Abolition of Slavery Act came into force on 1 August 1834, but first turned the freed into "apprentices" still tied to the estates. Sweden ended slavery on Saint-Barthélemy in 1847. On Danish St Croix, in July 1848, the enslaved forced the governor's hand by threatening to burn Frederiksted. The Netherlands abolished slavery on 1 July 1863, compensated the owners, and made many of the freed work on the plantations for ten more years under state supervision. In Spanish Cuba slavery was dismantled step by step and ended only in 1886. Brazil, by then an independent empire, declared slavery extinct in 1888.

The dates hide the people. Freedom came late, was often conditional, and in places like St Croix it was taken rather than given. The stories of [[danish-west-indies-1792|Denmark's ban]], [[pombal-1761|Portugal's laws]] and [[swedish-saint-barthelemy|Swedish Saint-Barthélemy]] show how.
