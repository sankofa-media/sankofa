In Florida, free Black people and people escaping slavery found refuge among the Seminoles, a Native nation, and joined forces with them. They became known as Black Seminoles, or Seminole Maroons.

When the United States set out to remove the Seminoles to the west, Black Seminoles fought the US Army in the Second Seminole War of 1835–1842. One of their leaders, John Horse, led Maroon fighters in Florida; the army offered the Maroons freedom if they surrendered, and in 1837 he agreed to move west to Indian Territory, today's Oklahoma.

Freedom there proved fragile: under Creek rule many risked being sold back into slavery. In 1849 John Horse led his people, together with Seminole allies, south into Mexico. The Mexican government gave them land in Coahuila, eventually at El Nacimiento, in exchange for guarding the frontier against Comanche and Apache raiders, and called them Mascogos.

Some later returned to Texas, and descendants of the Black Seminoles now live in Texas, Oklahoma and Coahuila. Their road to freedom ran through three countries, from Florida to a frontier village in Mexico.
