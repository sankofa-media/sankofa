Jamaican, which Jamaicans call Patwa, is the creole language of Jamaica. Most of its words come from English, but much of its grammar comes from the languages of West Africa; it was made by the enslaved Africans brought to the island. Nearly every Jamaican can speak and understand it, yet English is the only official language, and for generations Patwa was looked down on as coarse, a mark of the poor and the unschooled.

Louise Bennett, born in Kingston in 1919, wrote in it anyway. Her first book appeared in 1942, and from 1943 the Gleaner, the island's daily, carried her verse in a Sunday column. Audiences loved "Miss Lou", but critics faulted her "improper" language and treated her verse as mere dialect, not writing. Her collection Jamaica Labrish came out in 1966, and she spent decades on stage, radio and television before she died in Toronto in 2006.

In 2012 the Bible Society of the West Indies published Di Jamiekan Nyuu Testiment, the New Testament in Jamaican, translated with linguists from the University of the West Indies. Critics said Patwa would dilute scripture; the translators answered that people understand scripture best in their mother tongue. Bennett had insisted all along that Patwa was a language in its own right.
