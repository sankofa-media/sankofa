In the 1910s people with Hansen's disease, leprosy, in Hawaii were sent to the isolated settlement of Kalaupapa on Molokai, and the main remedy, oil pressed from the seeds of the chaulmoogra tree, was hard to give as a drug. Alice Augusta Ball was born in Seattle in 1892; her grandfather was the photographer J. P. Ball. She took degrees in pharmaceutical chemistry and in pharmacy at the University of Washington, then went to the College of Hawaii as a graduate student. In 1915 she became the first woman, and the first African American, to earn a master's degree in chemistry there.

Asked to make chaulmoogra oil injectable, she isolated the ethyl esters of its fatty acids, which could be injected and absorbed. She was 23. Soon afterwards she fell ill, possibly after inhaling chlorine in the laboratory, went home to Seattle and died on 31 December 1916, aged 24.

Arthur Dean, head of the college's chemistry department and later the university's president, carried the work on, and it became known as the "Dean method". Injections made by her technique remained the leading treatment until new drugs arrived in the 1940s. Researchers later restored her name; a chaulmoogra tree on the Mānoa campus now bears her plaque, and Hawaii marks 28 February as Alice Augusta Ball Day.
