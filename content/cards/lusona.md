Sona (singular lusona) are drawings traced on the ground by the Chokwe and their neighbours in eastern Angola and the borderlands of Zambia and Congo. They are not an alphabet for sounds but a tradition of signs made for telling. The drawer smooths the sand, presses a grid of evenly spaced dots into it with his fingertips, and then runs a line around and between the dots. Often it is a single unbroken line that closes on itself.

The drawings went with stories. A storyteller traced the figure as he told a fable, and the pattern helped teller and listeners hold the tale in memory: the line could become a fence, a river or a path, the dots the animals and people of the story.

When mathematicians looked closely, they found method. The mathematician Paulus Gerdes showed that the drawers valued symmetry and the single line as ideals, and that families of sona follow construction rules. In the simplest family, the plaited mat, the number of separate loops a grid needs equals the greatest common divisor of its rows and columns, the quantity computed by Euclid's algorithm.

In 2023 UNESCO added sona to its list of humanity's intangible heritage. Like the [[lukasa|Luba memory boards]] further north, they turn remembering into a pattern, here one that a single finger can draw.
