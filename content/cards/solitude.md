In 1794 revolutionary France abolished slavery, and the news was proclaimed in Guadeloupe that June. Eight years later Napoleon Bonaparte sent General Richepanse with an army to restore the old colonial order, and with it slavery. In May 1802, when the troops landed, the officer Louis Delgrès led free men of colour and the formerly enslaved in armed resistance.

Among those who took up arms was a woman known as Solitude, a few months pregnant. She was captured around 23 May 1802, as the resistance was crushed. A court sentenced her to death, but the execution waited for her child: she was put to death on 29 November 1802, the day after she gave birth.

That is nearly all the record holds. Her story survived through oral testimony that the historian Auguste Lacour wrote down in the nineteenth century, and in 1972 André Schwarz-Bart's novel La Mulâtresse Solitude gave her a full, imagined life.

The novel brought her out of oblivion. In 1999 the town of Les Abymes in Guadeloupe raised a statue to her, in 2020 Paris named a garden after her, and on 10 May 2022 a bronze statue of Solitude was unveiled there, the first statue of a Black woman in Paris.
