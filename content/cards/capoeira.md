Capoeira is a Brazilian art that is a fight and a dance at once, developed from traditions that enslaved Africans brought across the Atlantic. Two players meet in the middle of a circle, the roda, while the single-string berimbau sets the pace and everyone else sings and claps. Like [[palmares|Quilombo dos Palmares]], it belongs to the history of resistance to slavery in Brazil, and for a long time the state treated it as a crime: the penal code of 1890 punished capoeiragem, displays of agility in streets and squares, with two to six months in prison.

The art outlived the ban in its teachers. In the 1930s, in the time of President Getúlio Vargas, the persecution eased and a legal capoeira, taught in schools, took shape. Mestre Bimba's academy is often called the first formal school, while Mestre Pastinha kept the older Angola style. Their students carried capoeira across Brazil and then around the world. In 2014 UNESCO inscribed the capoeira circle as intangible heritage of humanity: what was once a crime had become a symbol of Brazil. It grew in the same Afro-Brazilian world as [[candomble|the candomblé houses of Salvador]] and [[samba|samba]].
