In 1944 Abbeville, in Henry County, Alabama, lived under Jim Crow segregation. On the night of 3 September Recy Taylor, a 24-year-old mother, was walking home from a service at the Rock Hill Holiness Church when a carload of armed white men forced her in at gunpoint. Six of them raped her in the woods, then left her on the highway.

She did not stay silent. She reported the attack, and the car's owner gave the sheriff the others' names. None of the six was arrested; her house was firebombed. The NAACP branch in Montgomery sent its investigator, Rosa Parks, who organised a Committee for Equal Justice for Mrs. Recy Taylor and won support across the country. Two all-white, all-male grand juries, in October 1944 and on 14 February 1945, refused to indict anyone.

After the historian Danielle McGuire retold the case in At the Dark End of the Street, the Alabama Legislature apologised to Taylor in 2011. She died in Abbeville in 2017, at 97. In Alabama, as the [[scottsboro|Scottsboro trials]] had shown, a white accuser could send Black youths to death row, while a Black woman's testimony could not bring white men to trial. Eleven years later Parks's own arrest set off the [[montgomery-bus-boycott|Montgomery bus boycott]].
