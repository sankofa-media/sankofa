Kriolu is the creole language of Cape Verde, an archipelago in the Atlantic off the coast of Senegal. It was forged from Portuguese and the languages of West Africa, and it is one of the oldest creole languages still spoken. Almost every Cape Verdean speaks it as a first language, and it varies from island to island. Portuguese remains the only official language, but everyday life is lived in Kriolu.

Its story begins on Santiago, the largest island. The first Portuguese settlers arrived there in the 1460s, and the town of Ribeira Grande, now called Cidade Velha, grew up on its southern coast. The colonists traded along the African coast for captives. Most of the enslaved Africans brought to Santiago were sold on to Europe and the Americas; those who stayed lived alongside the settlers, and out of that meeting of Portuguese and African speech the creole grew.

In 1998 Cape Verde adopted a unified alphabet for its language, ALUPEC, on a trial basis, and in 2009 confirmed it as the official way to write it. Today more Cape Verdeans live abroad than at home, many of them in the United States, and they carry Kriolu with them. A language made by the enslaved and their descendants became the everyday speech of a whole nation.
