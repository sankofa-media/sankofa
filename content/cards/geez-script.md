The Ge'ez script, also called Ethiopic, is the writing of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Each of its letters stands for a consonant, and small marks attached to the letter turn it into a syllable with a particular vowel, so a single letter has a whole family of forms. Unlike its Arabian parent, it runs left to right.

It grew out of the South Arabian alphabet and first wrote Ge'ez, the language of the kingdom of [[aksum-obelisk|Aksum]] in the northern Ethiopian highlands. The earliest Ge'ez inscriptions mark consonants only, as their Arabian models did. Then, in the 4th century, Aksum began writing the vowels too. Scholars commonly date this change to the reign of King Ezana, under whom Aksum turned to Christianity; inscriptions of his survive in both forms, with and without vowels.

Ge'ez itself stopped being spoken in the Middle Ages, but it lived on as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Its script did more than survive. It was adopted for Amharic, Tigre and other languages of the region.

From Ezana's stone inscriptions to the Amharic of today, the same letters have now been in use for some seventeen centuries.
