In the 1870s people were still bought and sold like goods in the markets of El Obeid and Khartoum, in Sudan. A girl born around 1869 in a village in Darfur was kidnapped at about nine and fed into that trade. The shock made her forget her own name; her captors called her Bakhita, Arabic for "fortunate". She was sold again and again.

In 1882, in Khartoum, the Italian consul Callisto Legnani bought her. When the Mahdist advance forced him home, she asked to go with him to Italy. There she became nanny to the small daughter of the Michieli family near Venice, and while the family went to Africa she was left with the Canossian Sisters in Venice. When Signora Michieli came back for her, Bakhita refused to leave.

She was baptised Josephine on 9 January 1890 and joined the order. For more than four decades she lived in its convent at Schio as cook, seamstress and doorkeeper, greeting the children at the gate; the town knew her as Madre Moretta, its little Black mother. She died at Schio on 8 February 1947. On 1 October 2000 Pope John Paul II canonised the girl whom slave traders had named "fortunate".
