Great Zimbabwe stands near today's Masvingo, on the plateau between the Zambezi and the Limpopo. Its name comes from Shona words for stone buildings, and it is the largest of the many stone-walled towns of that country. Ancestors of the Shona built its walls mainly between about 1250 and 1450, when it was the capital of a powerful kingdom and home to more than ten thousand people. Gold went out towards the [[swahili|Swahili]] ports of the Indian Ocean coast; Chinese and Persian ceramics and coins from Kilwa came back and have been found in the ruins.

Colonial settlers refused to believe that Africans had built it and credited the Phoenicians, or King Solomon's Ophir. In the early 20th century archaeologists, among them David Randall-MacIver and Gertrude Caton-Thompson, showed that it was African work. After white Rhodesia declared independence from Britain in 1965, Ian Smith's government kept the foreign-builders story alive and restricted what could be published about who had built the ruins. Peter Garlake, the Inspector of Monuments, who attributed Great Zimbabwe to the ancestors of local people, was forced out of the country and went to teach in Nigeria.

At independence in 1980 the new state named itself after the ruins. The walls that colonial rule had credited to strangers became the name of the nation.
