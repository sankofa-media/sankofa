In the 1890s mail reached remote settlements in Montana on "star routes": the Post Office paid private contractors to haul it by coach. One of those contractors was a formerly enslaved woman. Mary Fields was born in slavery in Tennessee around 1832, and little of her early life was ever written down. In the 1870s she worked for the Ursuline nuns at their convent in Toledo, Ohio. Around 1885 she followed them to St. Peter's Mission, outside the town of Cascade, where she gardened, did repairs and hauled supplies to the mission, work then counted as men's. After rumours of a gunfight, the bishop ordered the nuns to let her go.

In 1895 she won the contract to carry the mail between Cascade and the mission. She was in her sixties, and she kept the route for eight years, through rain and snow, the first African American woman to hold a star route contract. Her reliability earned her the nickname Stagecoach Mary. Later she ran a laundry in Cascade, and she died there in 1914.

Her contract is a rare paper trace of a woman born enslaved who, on the northern frontier, earned her own living on her own terms.
