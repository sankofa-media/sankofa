In 1784 Sweden acquired from France the small island of Saint-Barthélemy in the Lesser Antilles. It made the town of Gustavia a free port, and during the wars of 1793–1815 the town boomed as a neutral market where merchants of every flag did business.

People were part of that business. Enslaved Africans were bought and sold in Gustavia, and the port stayed important to the trade even after other powers had banned it. The Swedish crown wrote slave laws for the colony and used them to punish the enslaved harshly; by the early nineteenth century Black people, enslaved and free, were the majority of the islanders. Sweden abolished slavery on the island only in 1847, and in 1878 it handed Saint-Barthélemy back to France.

Then the story was nearly forgotten. Sweden's colonial archive stayed on the island, later moved to Guadeloupe and finally to the French colonial archives in Aix-en-Provence, where it lay in poor condition and almost out of researchers' reach. In the 2010s the historian Fredrik Thomasson of Uppsala University and his colleagues digitised it and opened it to research, and historians are now writing the lives of the island's Black majority back into Swedish history.
