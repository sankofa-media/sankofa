Fannie Lou Hamer was born in Mississippi in 1917, the youngest of twenty children of sharecroppers, and grew up on a cotton plantation in Sunflower County. She left school after the sixth grade. For nearly two decades she and her husband, Perry, worked on a plantation outside Ruleville, where she kept the time books.

In 1962 organisers of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee came to the county to register Black voters, and Hamer went to try. The owner fired her and forced her off the land. In 1963 police in Winona, Mississippi, arrested her with other activists, and she was beaten in the jail so badly that the injuries stayed with her for life. She joined the SNCC staff, and in 1964 she cast her first vote; she would twice run for Congress herself.

That summer she helped found the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party to challenge the state's all-white delegation at the Democratic convention in Atlantic City. Before the credentials committee she described her beating and ended: "I question America." Offered two seats, she answered: "We didn't come all this way for no two seats." She later sat as a delegate in 1968, founded the Freedom Farm cooperative in 1969 to feed poor families, and died in 1977. She had made the nation hear what voting cost.
