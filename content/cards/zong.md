In late 1781 the Liverpool-owned slave ship Zong, carrying about twice as many captives as it was built for, overshot Jamaica. Its master, Luke Collingwood, and the crew threw more than 130 Africans into the sea, some of them after rain had refilled the water the crew later claimed was running out. The owners claimed the dead on insurance as lost cargo.

In March 1783 a jury at London's Guildhall found for the owners. On the insurers' appeal in May, Lord Mansfield said the case was the same as if horses had been thrown overboard, and granted a new trial; there is no evidence it was held. No one was ever charged with murder.

{male} [[equiano|Olaudah Equiano]], who had bought his own freedom in 1766, carried news of the verdict to [[granville-sharp|Granville Sharp]]. Sharp had the May hearing taken down and then asked the Admiralty to try the crew for murder. Nothing came of it.

Outrage over the killings fed the abolition society founded in 1787, and abolitionists went on to make the slave ship itself an image in the [[brookes|Brookes]] print.

{male} In 1789 Equiano published his own story, The Interesting Narrative, and toured Britain and Ireland with it; it became a best-seller. A man once sold as cargo had made his own words evidence against the trade.

{female} In 2008 M. NourbeSe Philip, a Tobago-born poet and former Toronto lawyer, published Zong!. She allowed herself only the words of the two-page case report, Gregson v. Gilbert, and broke them apart across the page so that the document that counted people as cargo would remember them.

{female} Each year, around 29 November, when the killings began, readers gather to read the poem aloud together. A report about lost goods has become a rite of mourning for people.
