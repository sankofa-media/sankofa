The Hatata, Ge'ez for "inquiry", is a philosophical autobiography set in 17th-century Ethiopia. Its narrator, Zera Yacob, was born in the north of the country around 1600, into an age of religious strife, when Ethiopia's Orthodox Church was at odds with the Catholicism brought by foreign Jesuit missionaries.

He tells how he declared that no religion was more right than any other, how enemies denounced him to the king, and how he fled and lived alone in a cave. There, thinking by the light of reason, he worked out a philosophy of his own: a Creator who can be known through reason, women and men created equal, slavery condemned. When the violence ended he came back to the world, and a disciple, Walda Heywat, urged him to write it all down.

The text reached Europe in the mid-19th century through an Italian Capuchin missionary, Giusto da Urbino. In 1920 the Italian orientalist Carlo Conti Rossini declared it a forgery by Giusto himself, and the academic world accepted his verdict. Later scholars, among them Claude Sumner, who translated it into English in 1976, read it again as an Ethiopian work. The argument is not over, but the Hatata now has a place in the history of African philosophy.
