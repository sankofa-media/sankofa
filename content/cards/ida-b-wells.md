Ida B. Wells was a journalist in Memphis, Tennessee, in the decades after slavery, when white mobs across the South lynched Black people, killing them without trial, and excused it as punishment for the rape of white women. Born in Holly Springs, Mississippi, in 1862, she became co-owner of the Memphis Free Speech. In 1892 her friend Thomas Moss and his two partners in a Black grocery were lynched. In May she wrote: "Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread-bare lie that Negro men rape white women." Threats forced her out of Memphis and she could not go home; by her own account creditors sold the press while she was in New York.

She answered with evidence. Southern Horrors (1892) and A Red Record (1895) built their case on lynchings reported by white newspapers, above all the Chicago Tribune's annual tallies: "Out of their own mouths shall the murderers be condemned." In 1893 she took the campaign to Britain. In 1895 she married the Chicago lawyer Ferdinand Barnett; in Chicago she helped form the NAACP and a Black women's suffrage club, and she died there in 1931. In 2020 the Pulitzer board gave her a posthumous special citation for "her outstanding and courageous reporting". Her line runs from [[freedoms-journal|Freedom's Journal]] to [[emmett-till|Emmett Till]].
