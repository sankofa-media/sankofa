In eighteenth-century Britain, planters, merchants and ships' officers came home from the colonies with enslaved Africans as servants. When one escaped, the owner paid for a notice in the newspaper offering a reward and describing the runaway's looks, clothes, skills and scars. Written to recapture people, these adverts are often the only record that they existed.

One of them hunted Jamie. In 1750 Robert Shedden, a Scottish merchant in Virginia, bought a boy named Jamie, whose bill of sale survives, and sent him to Beith in Ayrshire to learn joinery, meaning to sell him back in Virginia as a skilled carpenter. In Beith Jamie was baptised and took the surname Montgomery. In 1756 Shedden had him taken to Port Glasgow to be shipped to Virginia; Jamie escaped, reached Edinburgh and found work as a carpenter. Shedden's adverts in the Glasgow and Edinburgh papers led to his arrest. Jamie went to law for his freedom in the Court of Session, but he died in the Edinburgh Tolbooth before it was heard.

From 2015 a University of Glasgow team combed newspapers printed in England and Scotland between 1700 and 1780 and gathered more than 800 such adverts. Read against their purpose, they are a record of people who refused to be owned, in Britain itself.
