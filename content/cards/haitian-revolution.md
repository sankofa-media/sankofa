Saint-Domingue, France's colony, was in 1789 the richest in the Americas, its sugar and coffee grown by hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans. In August 1791, after a ceremony at Bois Caïman, they rose, and under Toussaint Louverture fought off Spanish and British armies. In 1802 Napoleon's army under Charles Leclerc tried to restore slavery.

{male} Toussaint, born into slavery, had become the colony's governor and given it a constitution of his own. In June 1802 Leclerc's men seized him and put him on a ship for France. On board, he said that they had cut down only the trunk of the tree of liberty: it would grow again from the roots, for they were numerous and deep. Held without trial in the Fort de Joux in the Jura mountains, he died there in April 1803.

{female} Sanité Bélair was married to Toussaint's nephew Charles. When he rose against Leclerc in August 1802, she fought at his side. In September they were taken, and Jean-Jacques Dessalines, then a general in Leclerc's service, sent them to Le Cap. Accused also of having a sick young white man killed, she was sentenced, as a woman, to be beheaded; Charles, a soldier, to be shot. On 5 October she urged him to die bravely, refused the blindfold and would not bow to the block; she was shot. Haiti's ten-gourde note of 2004 bears her portrait.

Late in 1803 the rebels under Jean-Jacques Dessalines beat the French, and on 1 January 1804 he proclaimed Haiti. In 1825 France demanded 150 million francs, later cut to 90 million, for the former colonists; Haiti borrowed in France to pay and repaid that loan only in 1888. Maroons had held out in the hills of [[palmares|Brazil]] and [[nanny-of-the-maroons|Jamaica]]; here the enslaved took and kept a whole colony.
