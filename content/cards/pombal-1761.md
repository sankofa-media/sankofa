For three centuries Portugal brought enslaved Africans into the kingdom itself, first through [[lagos-1444|Lagos]] and then through Lisbon. On 19 September 1761 a royal alvará of King José I, issued under his minister Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, the future Marquis of Pombal, forbade ships from Africa, America and Asia to land any Black man or woman in Portugal. Anyone brought in from six months after its publication was to be "freed and manumitted" without any further letter or document.

The limits were written into the law. People already enslaved in Portugal stayed enslaved, and the law did not apply to Brazil or Africa; it even warned that it must not tempt the enslaved of the colonies to run away to Portugal. Slaveholders looked for gaps. Some kept mixed-race people in bondage, arguing that the law spoke only of Black men and women, until decrees of 1767 extended it to all.

In 1773 a second alvará declared that every child born from then on would be free, and freed those whose slavery went back to their great-grandmothers. Together the two laws set slavery in the kingdom itself on the road to its end, while the colonies went on as before.
