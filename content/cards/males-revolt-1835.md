In 1835 the Brazilian city of Salvador da Bahia ran on enslaved African labour, much of it in the streets: porters, tradesmen and vendors who handed their owners a share of what they earned. Among them were Muslims, and most of the Muslim rebels were Yoruba speakers, called Nagô in Bahia. In Bahia the Muslims were called malês. They met to pray and to learn to write passages of the Qur'an.

They set their rising for the early hours of Sunday 25 January, near the end of Ramadan. It was betrayed, and fighting broke out in the night. The rebels, in white Muslim robes, first attacked the jail that held a revered elderly leader, Pacífico Licutan, and failed to free him. About 600 people joined them as they fought through the streets. By dawn they were cornered at the cavalry barracks of Água de Meninos, and more than seventy rebels were killed.

Hundreds were arrested. The police seized Arabic writings and amulets holding verses of the Qur'an. Sentences ranged from flogging to deportation to Africa for freed men; four men were shot by a firing squad. Fear of another rising spread through the Empire of Brazil, and the repression of enslaved and freed Africans grew harsher.
