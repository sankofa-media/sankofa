In the 1440s Portuguese ships began landing captives from West Africa on the beach at Lagos, in the Algarve. Within a few decades Lisbon had become the main port through which enslaved Africans entered Portugal, and by the sixteenth century about one Lisboan in ten was Black, enslaved or free.

Black Lisboans made institutions of their own. They formed a Catholic brotherhood, Our Lady of the Rosary of Black Men, the first Black brotherhood in Lisbon and, it is believed, in Portugal, a place of shared devotion and mutual protection. As more of them won their freedom, a Black neighbourhood grew on the north-western edge of the city. It was called Mocambo, from an Angolan word, in Kimbundu, for a hideout or refuge.

The same streets held the harshest trace of the trade: a pit where the bodies of enslaved Africans were thrown. Its memory survives in a street name, Rua do Poço dos Negros, the Street of the Blacks' Pit, which runs through what was once Mocambo. Long before Rio or Charleston, a European capital already had a Black quarter with an African name.
