In the nineteenth century enslaved Africans were brought to the Ottoman Empire, and many worked on the cotton and tobacco farms around İzmir, on the Aegean coast. Freed people were settled in villages near İzmir and in Aydın province, and a few of those villages are still largely Black. Their descendants, estimated at anything up to 100,000, are known as Afro-Turks; neighbours have long called them "Arabs".

Each spring they held the Dana Bayramı, the Calf Festival, a celebration kept since the nineteenth century in and around İzmir. Then it stopped: banned in 1924, by one account, or faded away in the 1960s, by another.

Mustafa Olpak, whose family had come from Kenya by way of Crete, told their story in a memoir of 2005 that begins: "The first generation lives it, the second denies it, the third researches it." In 2006 he founded an Afro-Turkish association in Ayvalık, and in 2007 it revived the festival. Within a few years it drew hundreds of people.

Olpak has since died, but the festival goes on every spring. Ottoman slavery has been little studied, and the Afro-Turks now work with researchers to record their own history.
