During the American Revolution the British promised freedom to enslaved people who escaped from the rebels and joined them. After the war thousands of these Black Loyalists were shipped from New York to Nova Scotia in 1783, with certificates of freedom signed by General Samuel Birch. Near Shelburne a site was laid out for them and named after him: Birchtown.

By January 1784 Birchtown had about 1,500 people, the largest free Black settlement in North America. Among them was Boston King, a carpenter who had escaped slavery near Charleston and arrived with his wife in August 1783; in Birchtown he became a Methodist preacher. But land grants were slow and small, and in July 1784 white Loyalists rioted in Shelburne, tearing down Black homes. King later recalled the famine of 1789 and the "poverty and distress" around him.

In 1791 John Clarkson of the Sierra Leone Company came recruiting. On 15 January 1792 a fleet of fifteen ships left Halifax for Sierra Leone, carrying many of Birchtown's people, who built the new colony of Freetown. Birchtown largely emptied, though some families stayed. Canada's Historic Sites and Monuments Board calls it the largest and most influential of the Black Loyalist settlements. It was a free town that its people built themselves, and then chose to leave.
