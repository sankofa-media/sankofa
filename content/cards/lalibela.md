Lalibela is a town high in the mountains of northern Ethiopia and one of the great pilgrimage places of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. It bears the name of Lalibela, a medieval king of the Zagwe dynasty, and tradition holds that he set out to make here a new Jerusalem.

Its eleven churches were cut down into the ground, not built up. Masons worked a ridge of soft volcanic tuff, freeing blocks of living rock and then carving them inside and out, so that each church is a single piece of stone. Some stand in deep pits reached by trenches; others are cut into a cliff face. The largest of them is Biete Medhane Alem, the House of the Saviour of the World, and names across the site, such as Golgotha and the Tomb of Adam, echo the Holy Land.

Tradition says the churches were all finished within King Lalibela's reign. Archaeologists are less sure. David Phillipson argued that the oldest rock-cut features may be five centuries older than the king, and a French team reading the cuts, openings and spoil heaps asks whether this was one grand plan or a sacred centre reshaped over a long time. Either way, the churches are alive: priests serve them, and pilgrims still walk the carved passages between them.
