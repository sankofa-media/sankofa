Afonso I, born Mvemba a Nzinga, ruled the kingdom of Kongo in the first half of the 16th century from Mbanza Kongo, later São Salvador, in today's northern Angola. His father, King Nzinga a Nkuwu, had been baptised João I in 1491, after Kongo's first contacts with Portugal. Afonso made Catholicism the state religion, built churches and schools, and sent young nobles to study in Lisbon.

The Portuguese alliance brought priests and craftsmen, and a growing demand for slaves. Afonso did not reject slavery as such: war captives were sold. But in July 1526 a letter in his name told King João III that merchants were seizing "sons of the land", the sons of his nobles and even his relatives, until the country was being emptied, and that Kongo wanted only priests, schoolteachers, and wine and flour for Mass. In another letter that year he described free men kidnapped at night, put in irons and branded, and a new law: any white buyer had first to let three named officials of his court check that a captive was not a free person.

The trade kept growing, and in the end it destabilised Kongo and its neighbours. Afonso died in the 1540s. His letters let a king of the 1520s speak for his own subjects against the Atlantic trade.
