Eugene Bullard was born in Columbus, Georgia, in 1895. After seeing his father nearly lynched, he ran away from home in 1906, and in 1912 he stowed away on a German merchant ship bound for Aberdeen, Scotland. He boxed for money and played in a variety troupe in Britain and across Europe, and in November 1913 he fought in Paris for the first time.

When the First World War began he joined the French Foreign Legion. Twice wounded, decorated with the Croix de Guerre for Verdun and no longer fit for the infantry, he asked to be trained as an airman. In 1917 he flew about twenty combat missions for France, usually with a pet monkey along: the first African American combat pilot. When the United States entered the war its air service refused him, and he was taken off flying, for reasons the sources dispute.

Between the wars he ran nightclubs in Montmartre, among them his own, L'Escadrille, where Josephine Baker was among the crowd he knew. In the late 1930s he spied on German customers for French counter-intelligence. After France fell he reached New York and took odd jobs, among them running an elevator at Rockefeller Center. In 1959 France made him a knight of the Legion of Honour. He died in New York in 1961.
