The Ishango bone is a small handle of bone, about ten centimetres long, found at Ishango, in the east of what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, close to the border with Uganda. The Belgian archaeologist Jean de Heinzelin dug it up in the 1950s, and it is dated to more than 20,000 years ago. A splinter of quartz fixed in one end shows that it once held a cutting edge.

Along its length run three columns of grouped notches. In Scientific American in 1962 de Heinzelin noted that one column holds 11, 13, 17 and 19, the prime numbers between 10 and 20, and that another pairs 3 with 6 and 4 with 8, as if doubling. He suggested an arithmetical game. Alexander Marshack later read the marks as a calendar. Vladimir Pletser and Dirk Huylebrouck proposed instead a count that mixed tens and twelves. The mathematician Olivier Keller answers that several notches are worn or barely visible, which makes any reading in numbers suspect.

The bone is on show at the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences in Brussels, and its admirers call it humanity's oldest mathematical tool. Whatever the notches meant, a human hand cut them one by one in the heart of Africa more than twenty thousand years ago.
