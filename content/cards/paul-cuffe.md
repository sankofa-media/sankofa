In revolutionary Massachusetts a free Black man could be taxed yet denied the vote. Paul Cuffe was born on Cuttyhunk Island in 1759, one of ten children of Kofi, a formerly enslaved Asante man, and Ruth Moses, a Wampanoag woman. He took his father's first name as his surname and went to sea as a teenager on whaling ships. In 1780 he and a brother petitioned Massachusetts against being taxed without a vote. He went on to buy and build ships, trading along the coast and abroad. A Quaker and an opponent of slavery, he dreamed of a Black settlement in Africa built on emigration and trade, and in 1811 he sailed his brig Traveller, with an all-Black crew, to Freetown in Sierra Leone and on to England. Coming home in April 1812, he lost ship and cargo to customs officers enforcing the ban on British goods. On 2 May he went to the White House, and President James Madison had them returned. After the War of 1812 Cuffe carried 38 Black American settlers to Freetown, landing on 3 February 1816. In January 1817 a mass meeting of free Black men at [[jarena-lee|Bethel Church]] in Philadelphia rejected emigration to Africa, and that September Cuffe died. Henry Highland Garnet and Martin Delany later took up his dream.
