In 1802 Napoleon sent an army to retake Saint-Domingue, and Toussaint Louverture was seized and shipped to France. The [[haitian-revolution|revolution]] went on. In May 1803 its leading generals met in congress at Arcahaie and united behind Jean-Jacques Dessalines with a single aim, independence. Haitian tradition fills in a scene. Dessalines tore the white band out of the French tricolour, and his goddaughter Catherine Flon, a seamstress who also nursed the wounded, stitched the blue and red together. The archive is almost silent about her. The historian Philippe Girard finds that documents support few details of the flag story and doubts that Flon existed at all; Nicole Willson replies that, named or not, at least one woman must have sewn that flag, and that the story keeps the work of revolutionary women in view. The story won. Haiti became independent in 1804, 18 May is celebrated as Flag Day, and Flon's likeness is on the ten-gourde note. Haitian artists keep painting her at her needle.
