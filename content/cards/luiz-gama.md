In nineteenth-century Brazil slavery was legal, but an 1831 law had banned the Atlantic slave trade and declared Africans brought in after it free; people went on being enslaved in violation of it. Luiz Gama was born free in Salvador, Bahia, in 1830, the son of an African woman, Luiza Mahin, and a Portuguese nobleman. When the boy was ten, his father sold him into slavery to settle a gambling debt, and he was taken to São Paulo as a household slave. At seventeen he learned to read and write; at about eighteen he proved that he had been born free and left captivity. The São Paulo law school shut its doors to a Black man, so Gama studied law on his own and became a rábula, someone allowed to plead in court without a degree. He made the 1831 law his weapon, arguing case after case that the people in front of the judge had been enslaved illegally, and he won freedom for more than 500 of them. He died in 1882, six years before Brazil abolished slavery. In 2015, 133 years after his death, the Brazilian Bar Association made him a lawyer, and in 2018 a federal law entered his name in the Book of Heroes of the Fatherland.
