Sheng is the street language of Nairobi. Linguists describe it as a young, urban form of Kenyan Swahili: Swahili grammar carries a vocabulary taken from English and from the home languages of the city's peoples, and constantly renewed. A man is a msee; old words keep acquiring new meanings, so that the code stays a step ahead of outsiders. The name is usually read as a blend of Swahili and English.

Sheng grew up in Eastlands, the low-income estates that colonial Nairobi had set aside for African workers. Families from many peoples lived there side by side, speaking their own languages at home, Swahili outside and English at school, and it was probably their children who first mixed the three into a common tongue. When that happened is disputed. From the estates Sheng spread through the street economy, among shoeshine boys, hawkers and the parking boys who guarded cars for tips, and then reached wealthier teenagers, including pupils of Nairobi's private schools.

By the early 2000s it was heard in markets, in homes, on radio and television and in advertisements, and young people in other towns and in the countryside were picking it up.

Sheng shows the poorest neighbourhoods of a capital setting the speech of a whole generation.
