Sheng is the street language of Nairobi. Linguists describe it as a young, urban form of Kenyan Swahili: Swahili grammar carries a vocabulary taken from English and from the home languages of the city's peoples, Luo and Gikuyu among them. The words never sit still: new ones appear all the time, and old ones keep acquiring new meanings. The name is usually read as a blend of Swahili and English.

Sheng grew up in Eastlands, the low-income estates that colonial Nairobi had set aside for African workers. Families from many peoples lived there side by side, and out of their home languages, Swahili and English a common street code emerged. When that happened is disputed. From the estates Sheng spread through the street economy, among hawkers and street children, and in time, carried above all by music, it was taken up by young people of every class, pupils of private schools included.

By the early 2000s it was heard on radio and television, and young people in other towns and in the countryside were picking it up.

Sheng shows the poorest neighbourhoods of a capital setting the speech of a whole generation.
