Kenwood House, on Hampstead Heath in north London, was in the 1760s the home of Lord Mansfield, Lord Chief Justice of England. From about 1766 two of his great-nieces grew up there together. One was Lady Elizabeth Murray. The other was Dido Elizabeth Belle, born in 1761 to Mansfield's nephew, the naval officer John Lindsay, and Maria Belle, an enslaved woman.

Dido was educated as a gentlewoman. As an adult she ran Kenwood's dairy and poultry yard and helped her great-uncle with his letters. His court, meanwhile, heard the slavery cases of the age: in the Somerset case of 1772 Mansfield ruled that an enslaved man could not be forcibly sent out of England, and in 1783 he presided over the [[zong|Zong]] insurance case. In the late 1770s a painter portrayed the two cousins side by side on one canvas.

Mansfield died in 1793. His will confirmed Dido's freedom and left her £500 and an annuity of £100. Later that year she married John Davinier, a Frenchman, and they had three sons. She died in 1804, aged 43.

The double portrait, now attributed to the Scottish painter David Martin, hangs at Scone Palace in Scotland. It is one of the first paintings to show a Black woman as the equal of a white one.
