Henry Ossawa Tanner was born in Pittsburgh in 1859, the son of a prominent churchman, grew up in Philadelphia and studied painting there under Thomas Eakins. In 1891 he left for Paris and the Académie Julian. Two years later, in poor health, he came back to Philadelphia, to a country whose popular pictures of a Black man with a [[banjo|banjo]] were mostly drawn by white artists: a carefree figure of fun, made to be laughed at.

Tanner painted the opposite. In The Banjo Lesson, finished in 1893, an elderly man holds the instrument steady while a boy learns to play. Nothing in the scene is a joke: the picture is about patience, dignity and knowledge passing from one generation to the next.

In 1894 the painting was accepted at the Paris Salon, the most prestigious juried exhibition in the city, and that same year it went to the Hampton Institute in Virginia, whose museum still owns it.

Tanner soon returned to France for good and won an international reputation with religious paintings; he never went back to American scenes, and he died in Paris in 1937. The Banjo Lesson remains his best-known picture and a quiet answer to the caricatures.
