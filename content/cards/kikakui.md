Kikakui is a syllabary for Mende, a language of southern and eastern Sierra Leone and neighbouring Liberia. Each sign stands for a whole syllable, a consonant together with its vowel, and lines run from right to left, as in the Arabic script its makers knew. The name is simply the sound of the first three signs: ki, ka, ku.

A first set of signs was drawn by Mohamed Turay. His student Kisimi Kamara reworked and enlarged it in the early 1920s, until it held nearly two hundred signs, many of them close in form to the [[vai-script|Vai script]] of neighbouring Liberia. From the 1920s Kamara spread Kikakui beyond his home chiefdom, and for two decades Mende people wrote letters and kept records in a script of their own.

In the 1940s the British set up a Protectorate Literacy Bureau in Bo to teach Mende in Latin letters, and Kikakui slowly fell out of use. In 2014 Unicode encoded it in version 7.0, so the old signs can now be typed and searched.

Kikakui shows that Mende writing in a Mende-made script was already part of daily life before the colonial state taught the language in Latin letters.
