Every November the Netherlands stages the arrival of Sinterklaas, the bishop of its children's December feast, with his helpers, the Zwarte Pieten, "Black Petes". Traditionally Piet was played mostly by white performers in blackface make-up.

On 12 November 2011, at the national arrival of Sinterklaas in Dordrecht, the artist Quinsy Gario and a fellow activist wore T-shirts reading "Zwarte Piet is racisme" and were arrested for disturbing public order. In October 2014 the National Ombudsman ruled that the arrests had been unlawful, disproportionately violent and a breach of their fundamental rights.

In August 2015 the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination wrote that Black Pete is sometimes portrayed in ways that reflect negative stereotypes of people of African descent and is experienced by many of them as a vestige of slavery. It asked the state to promote the removal of those features and to investigate and prosecute attacks on peaceful protesters.

Change came through television and town halls, not law. The broadcaster behind the children's news show Sinterklaasjournaal introduced "sooty" Petes, their faces only smudged from chimneys, in 2014; from 2019 every Piet at the televised national arrival was sooty, and more and more towns followed. In June 2020 Prime Minister Mark Rutte told parliament that his own view of Zwarte Piet had changed greatly.
