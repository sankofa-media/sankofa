In 1955 the city buses of Montgomery, Alabama, were segregated by law, and Black passengers could be ordered to give up their seats to white riders. The Women's Political Council, Black professional women led by the college teacher Jo Ann Robinson, had warned the mayor in 1954 that a boycott might come. Earlier in 1955 police arrested fifteen-year-old Claudette Colvin and eighteen-year-old Mary Louise Smith for keeping their seats. On 1 December Rosa Parks refused too and was arrested.

That night Robinson and two students mimeographed tens of thousands of leaflets at Alabama State College calling for a one-day boycott on 5 December. Almost no Black passengers rode. That afternoon local leaders formed the Montgomery Improvement Association and elected as president a 26-year-old pastor new to town, [[martin-luther-king|Martin Luther King Jr.]] People walked or shared rides in an organised carpool, and women did much of the work that kept the boycott going. King's house was bombed; dozens of leaders were indicted under an old conspiracy law.

Four women, Colvin and Smith among them, sued in federal court. In November 1956 the Supreme Court upheld the ruling that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and on 21 December Black Montgomery boarded desegregated buses. The boycott made King a national figure and became the model for nonviolent campaigns across the South.
