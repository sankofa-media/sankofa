From 1672 Denmark–Norway held Caribbean colonies: St Thomas, then St John in 1718 and St Croix in 1733. Their sugar was grown by enslaved Africans, and ships under the Danish flag carried more than 100,000 captives across the Atlantic from Danish forts on the West African coast.

On 16 March 1792 Denmark became the first country to ban the transatlantic slave trade. The ordinance did not touch slavery itself, and it would take effect only in 1803. The delay was deliberate: planters were meant to stock up so that the enslaved population could sustain itself. In the decade of grace the Danish trade reached its peak. Economics, not only conscience, drove the ban; the Danish trade was losing money.

Slavery on the islands lasted another half-century, and in the end the enslaved forced its end. In early July 1848 several hundred gathered at Frederiksted on St Croix, led by a man known as General Buddhoe, and threatened to burn the town unless they were freed. Governor-General Peter von Scholten proclaimed all the unfree in the Danish West Indies free. Thirty years later the island's workers rose again in [[fireburn-queens|the Fireburn of 1878]].
