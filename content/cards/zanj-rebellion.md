In the ninth century Basra, in southern Iraq, was a city of the Abbasid caliphate. Around it lay salt marshes that landowners wanted turned into farmland, and the work was done by thousands of enslaved Africans, known as the Zanj, who dug away the salty crust of the soil.

In about 869 a man named Ali ibn Muhammad came into their camps and called them to revolt. The rebels did more than raid: they built a state of their own, with a standing army, a capital and their own coins, and for fourteen years they held southern Iraq against the caliph's armies.

The end came from al-Muwaffaq, the Abbasid commander who took the war in hand, besieged the rebel capital and wore it down. In 883 Ali ibn Muhammad was killed in battle and the revolt collapsed. Many of the Zanj fighters were taken into the Abbasid army instead of being returned to their masters.

For centuries the rising was remembered mostly as a stain in the chronicles of its enemies. In Basra today Black Iraqis, descendants of East Africans, tell it as the story of their own ancestors: see [[afro-iraqis-basra|the Afro-Iraqis of Basra]].
