Kilwa Kisiwani is a small island off the coast of southern Tanzania. From around the 10th century a Swahili trading city grew there, and by the 13th and 14th centuries it was one of the great ports of the East African coast, a link in the Indian Ocean trade that ran from the African interior to Arabia, India and beyond. Gold from inland passed through its hands.

The traveller Ibn Battuta sailed in during the early 1330s and found a very fine, well-built town. Its Great Mosque, roofed with domes and vaults, still stands, as do the ruins of Husuni Kubwa, a great palace of the 14th century. And Kilwa struck its own copper coins.

In 1944 Maurie Isenberg, an Australian serviceman posted on the Wessel Islands off Arnhem Land, picked five small copper coins out of the sand, along with four Dutch ones. Decades later they were identified as coins of the sultanate of Kilwa, on the other side of the Indian Ocean. A shipwreck or a forgotten trade route may have carried them; the question is still open.

The Portuguese seized Kilwa in 1505, and the city slowly declined. Its ruins are now a World Heritage site, the shell of an African port that once reached halfway across the world.
