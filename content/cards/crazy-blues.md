"Crazy Blues" was recorded in New York on 10 August 1920 by Mamie Smith and her Jazz Hounds, a band of Black musicians, and OKeh released it that autumn as OKeh 4169. The song was Perry Bradford's "Harlem Blues" under a new title.

{male} Bradford had come up through Black minstrel and vaudeville troupes and settled in New York as a songwriter and pianist. From 1918 to 1920 he badgered the executives of Victor, Columbia and OKeh to take a chance on his singers. Victor tested Mamie Smith and passed. Fred Hager, OKeh's recording director, said yes, although white correspondents had warned him against recording Black performers. Smith's first session, in February 1920, held two of Bradford's songs that were not [[blues|blues]]; they sold well enough for Hager to want a blues next, and Bradford brought "Harlem Blues". He told the story of that session in his autobiography, Born with the Blues (1965), and died in 1970.

{female} Smith was a veteran of Black show business long before the studio. Born in Cincinnati, she toured as a child with the Four Dancing Mitchells, came to New York as a chorus dancer with the Smart Set, a Black vaudeville troupe, and turned singer in Harlem. Her first session, in February 1920, gave her two songs that were not [[blues|blues]]; in August she sang the blues. "Crazy Blues" made her a star who toured, recorded and later appeared in films. She died poor in 1946 and was buried in an unmarked grave in Frederick Douglass Memorial Park on Staten Island. In 2014 a campaign by the music writer Michael Cala gave it a headstone.

The record showed the industry a market it had overlooked and began the "race records" business: through the 1920s companies hurried to record Black women singing the [[blues|blues]], and [[jazz|jazz]] followed them onto disc. Their line runs from Mamie Smith to [[big-mama-thornton|Willie Mae "Big Mama" Thornton]].
