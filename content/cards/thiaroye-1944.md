The tirailleurs sénégalais were France's colonial infantry, recruited across French West Africa. When Germany invaded France in 1940, they fought in its defence, and many were taken prisoner and spent the war in German camps in occupied France. Freed in 1944, they were shipped home and held at the military camp of Thiaroye, on the outskirts of Dakar.

Even before leaving France they had been complaining about their pay, the back wages and rights owed to them as former prisoners of war. At Thiaroye the anger grew, and the colonial authorities treated it as a mutiny. On 1 December 1944 French troops turned their guns on the West African soldiers. The official toll was 35 dead; other estimates run as high as 400.

For eighty years France did not call it a massacre. On the eve of the anniversary, in November 2024, President Emmanuel Macron wrote to Senegal's president, Bassirou Diomaye Faye, recognising for the first time that a massacre had taken place, set off by the soldiers' demand for the wages owed to them. Days later Senegal marked the eightieth anniversary of the killings.
