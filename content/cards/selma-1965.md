In 1965 only about 2 per cent of Black residents of Dallas County, Alabama, were on the voting rolls. In the county seat, Selma, local activists, SNCC and Martin Luther King Jr.'s Southern Christian Leadership Conference ran a voting-rights campaign from January and met mass arrests by Sheriff Jim Clark. On 18 February, at a night march in nearby Marion, a state trooper shot Jimmie Lee Jackson; he died eight days later.

In response, about 600 people set out on Sunday 7 March to walk 54 miles to the state capitol in Montgomery, led by Hosea Williams and John Lewis. On the Edmund Pettus Bridge troopers and mounted possemen attacked them with clubs and tear gas; more than fifty ended up in hospital. Television carried "Bloody Sunday" nationwide. Two days later King led some 2,000 people back to the bridge, knelt to pray and turned them around. That night white men beat the Unitarian minister James Reeb, who died of his injuries.

Federal judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. then authorised the march. About 8,000 people left Selma on 21 March, and on 25 March 25,000 gathered before the capitol. That night Klansmen murdered Viola Liuzzo, a volunteer driving marchers home. The march pushed Congress to act: on 6 August President Johnson signed the [[civil-rights-act-1964|Voting Rights Act]].
