In the mid-1970s a home console played only its built-in games. Gerald "Jerry" Lawson grew up in Queens building ham radios and selling walkie-talkies, and became one of the first Black engineers in Silicon Valley and an early Homebrew Computer Club member.

At Fairchild he built a coin-operated game, Demolition Derby, in his garage on Fairchild's F8 microprocessor and tested it in a pizza parlour. Put in charge of Fairchild's video products, he led the team behind the Channel F (1976), the first home console to take interchangeable cartridges, which Fairchild called Videocarts. The Atari 2600 soon eclipsed it.

Around 1980 Lawson left to found Video Soft, which made Atari 2600 software and released a Color Bar Generator, a cartridge for servicing television sets. Game developers honoured him as a pioneer in March 2011; weeks later he died of complications of diabetes, aged 70. The cartridge transformed the industry, and consoles kept the idea for decades.
