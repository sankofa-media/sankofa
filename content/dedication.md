This project is dedicated to my academic supervisor, Irina Mikhailovna Udler. For many years she studied the slave narrative, above all the work of Olaudah Equiano, and she was one of the leading scholars of the subject in the world. She also remained for me a model of the academic way of doing research: thoroughness, objectivity and, in the good sense, an obsession with scholarship and with her own subject. She was a wonderful teacher, and "the Udler list" is still a byword among philologists in Chelyabinsk.

I was always an unreliable slacker, and she could not accept that attitude, so at times I was hard work for her, while I loved being her student, because every time she taught me something that later helped me in life. Once, for example, I brought her a term paper with "Tukaev Timur" on the cover. I stood by her desk; she sat, quickly looked over the cover, the layout and the contents, turned to me and said: "Timur, in the Western academic tradition one writes one's first name before the surname; it is a sign of self-respect. Timur, you do respect yourself, don't you?" Ever since, I have always written my first name first and my surname second. It's like firmware.

It was through her, too, that I first came to Black musical tradition: she loved spirituals, gospel, blues and jazz. She was probably the most stylish sixty-year-old woman in Chelyabinsk :) And that style of hers was an inner one; outwardly she was always reserved, calm and strict, and she did not show her feelings. Once we were organising her jubilee at the university, and that was when I learned about her taste in music and put together a long playlist, so that colleagues could pick her up from home by car and drive her to the university with that music playing. That was also when I myself got to know the tradition more deeply. Nina Simone's "Sinnerman", for example, is a song I came to love right then. Since then, bit by bit, I have listened to one thing or another. Of course, I was never a serious connoisseur of this music and knew it rather superficially, as individual pieces.

But now and then feelings broke through her reserve. Once, for example, someone called her a talented scholar, and she said tenderly that compared with her husband (who had passed away by then) she knew and could do nothing.

I did not hand in my fourth-year term paper to her; I fooled around the whole year. And in my fifth year I did not even go to see her about my thesis, because I was too ashamed :) So, when the pre-defences and all the rest were already over, I turned up at her department for the first time and handed her my thesis. She asked, more or less: you didn't even give me last year's term paper, we never discussed your thesis (in fact we had, but back in the second year) — what exactly are you counting on? The thesis defences are next week. I mumbled something like: "Yes, I understand; if you throw me out, what can I do." Anyway, she took my thesis a little squeamishly, and the next day she called, her voice full of enthusiasm, and said some things needed reworking and the formatting needed improving. For some reason she liked it, and at that moment any personal grievances (which in such a situation would have been entirely justified) faded into the background for her. The scholar in her won at once :)

I was also told how she and her husband (they had studied philology together) once heard that a bookshop had received a collected edition of Remarque. They ran to buy it and each bought a set. Then they walked out of the shop, pleased, and her future husband suddenly said: "Ira, why did we buy two sets? We're going to get married anyway." That was the moment they realised, and said out loud, that from then on they were together to the end.

All in all, Irina Mikhailovna was an extraordinary woman. All the modern movie nonsense about strong women did not apply to her: she never put herself on show, she was modest and immersed in her work, and yet she was truly strong and dignified in everything she did, even aristocratic.

Why is all of this packed into Kubernetes? There is a reason for that, too. It was Irina Mikhailovna who did much to bring me to what I do now. At sixty she taught herself computers from scratch, and so well that she could easily reinstall drivers, Windows and the like. She was a very advanced user, and shortly before she passed away she was teaching students how to set up a VPN and get around blocks :) They didn't know how; she did. She literally made me type my first term paper on a computer, because the subject demanded it. That is how my path began, a path that has so far led me to open source and Linux, and then to Aenix and Cozystack. And when, after the army, I came back to the university to teach, we swapped computer magazines and all sorts of tips with each other, and talked for hours about operating system tweaks and other computer things.

And yes, my term papers and my thesis with her were on Milorad Pavić. So the ideas from his "Dictionary of the Khazars" are here for a reason.

So this project is dedicated to her.
